MODULE 1

What "Outstanding" Means

Learning objectives

  • Define precisely when a transaction is "outstanding" and when it stops being so.
  • Understand the address-vs-response decoupling that makes it possible.
  • Read the "outstanding window" of a transaction on a timeline.
  • Distinguish outstanding from the related ideas of pipelining and bursts.
Analogy — ordering at a busy coffee shop. You place your order and pay (the request is accepted). You don't stand frozen at the till until your drink is made — you step aside, and the next customer orders. Your drink is "outstanding": ordered, paid for, not yet delivered. The shop may have ten drinks outstanding at once. A transaction is outstanding in exactly the same sense — accepted by the system, not yet completed.

The precise definition

A transaction is outstanding from the cycle its address is accepted (the address-channel handshake completes) until the cycle its final response is accepted. For a read, that final point is the RLAST beat being accepted on the R channel. For a write, it's the single B response being accepted on the B channel.

Outstanding window.
Read: starts when ARVALID && ARREADY, ends when RVALID && RREADY && RLAST.
Write: starts when AWVALID && AWREADY, ends when BVALID && BREADY.
The number of outstanding transactions is how many have started but not yet ended at a given moment.

What makes it possible: decoupled channels

Recall that AXI's address channels (AR/AW) are completely separate from its data/response channels (R/B), each with its own handshake. Because the address handshake completes independently of the response, a manager can get an address accepted and immediately move on to issue the next address — long before the first response arrives. Each accepted-but-not-yet-answered request is one outstanding transaction.

Outstanding vs pipelining vs bursts — three different ideas

Fresh engineers routinely conflate these. They are related but distinct:

IdeaWhat it isGranularity
BurstOne address, many data beats in a single transaction.Within one transaction.
OutstandingSeveral whole transactions accepted but not yet completed.Across transactions.
PipeliningThe general technique of overlapping phases; outstanding is AXI's expression of it at the transaction level.Conceptual umbrella.

So a single 16-beat read burst is one transaction (it can be outstanding), while issuing four separate reads before any response returns is four outstanding transactions.

Why a verification engineer must nail this

Almost every interesting interconnect bug involves multiple things in flight at once. If your testbench only ever has one transaction outstanding, you are testing a tiny, unrealistic corner of the design and the hardest bugs — buffer overflows, ID-tracking errors, ordering violations — can never appear. Your driver must be able to create many outstanding transactions, and your scoreboard must be able to track them. That is the entire subject of this course.

Activity — count the outstanding transactions

A manager issues, in order: read A (address accepted at cycle 2), read B (accepted cycle 4), read C (accepted cycle 6). Responses complete: A at cycle 12, B at cycle 9, C at cycle 15. What is the maximum number of transactions outstanding at any one time, and in which cycle range does that maximum occur?

Draw three horizontal bars on a timeline, one per transaction, from its accept cycle to its complete cycle. Then scan left-to-right and count how many bars overlap at each cycle. The maximum overlap is the answer.

Windows: A = [2,12], B = [4,9], C = [6,15].

  • Cycles 2–3: only A → 1 outstanding.
  • Cycles 4–5: A, B → 2 outstanding.
  • Cycles 6–9: A, B, C → 3 outstanding (the maximum).
  • After B completes at 9: A, C → 2. After A completes at 12: C → 1. After 15: 0.

Maximum = 3, during cycles 6–9. Notice B completed (cycle 9) before A (cycle 12) even though A was issued first — that's legal out-of-order completion, which only matters because multiple transactions were outstanding. This is the exact situation your scoreboard must handle, and we build it in Modules 6–7.

When does a read transaction stop being outstanding?

A read is outstanding until its final data beat (the one with RLAST) is accepted. The first beat doesn't end it — a burst can have many beats. Asserting ARVALID is before acceptance, and other transactions' timing is irrelevant to this one's window.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Define the outstanding window in terms of the exact handshake edges (address-accept to final-response-accept) in your monitor.

Why: a precise start/end is the basis for the outstanding counter, depth coverage, and timeout logic. Fuzzy definitions produce off-by-one tracking bugs.

✗ Common mistake

Treating a burst's individual beats as separate outstanding transactions.

Why it hurts: a burst is ONE transaction; counting beats inflates your outstanding count and corrupts depth checks and coverage.

✓ Good practice

Always design the testbench to drive more than one transaction outstanding from day one.

Why: single-outstanding testing hides the entire class of concurrency bugs that outstanding capability exists to expose.

✗ Common mistake

Assuming "outstanding" and "burst length" are the same knob.

Why it hurts: they stress completely different RTL — burst length exercises beat counters, outstanding exercises transaction-tracking buffers. Confusing them leaves one untested.

MODULE 2

Why Outstanding Transactions Exist

Learning objectives

  • Explain how outstanding transactions hide memory latency.
  • Contrast a blocking (one-at-a-time) manager with a non-blocking one on a timeline.
  • Reason about throughput vs latency intuitively.
  • Connect the performance argument to why verification must stress depth.
Analogy — posting letters and waiting for replies. Imagine you must mail ten questions, each to a different office, and each reply takes a week. If you mail one letter and wait for its reply before mailing the next, you spend ten weeks. If you mail all ten at once on day one, every office works in parallel and you have all ten replies in about one week. The reply time (latency) didn't change — but your throughput improved tenfold by having ten "letters outstanding."

The latency problem

A read to DRAM can take many tens of cycles to return. If a manager issues one read and then idles until the data comes back, the bus and the manager sit doing nothing for almost the entire latency. With deep memory and fast clocks, that wasted time dominates — you could be achieving a tiny fraction of the bus's potential throughput.

Blocking vs non-blocking — a side-by-side timeline

The picture below tells the whole story. Top: a blocking manager waits for each response before issuing the next request. Bottom: a non-blocking manager keeps several requests outstanding, overlapping their latencies.

Throughput vs latency — the intuition

Two different quantities are at play. Latency is how long one transaction takes end-to-end; outstanding does not reduce it. Throughput is how many transactions complete per unit time; outstanding dramatically increases it. The rough relationship every DV engineer should carry in their head:

Max useful outstanding ≈ latency × throughput.
To keep a pipe with, say, 50-cycle latency fully busy at one transaction per cycle, you need about 50 transactions outstanding. Fewer, and the pipe starves; the bus goes idle waiting.

This is why real designs advertise an outstanding capability (e.g. "up to 32 outstanding reads") — it is sized to cover the expected latency. And it is exactly why your verification must drive up to and beyond that number: the design's buffers are dimensioned for it, and the bugs live at the limit.

The verification consequence

The performance argument hands you your test plan. If the design claims N outstanding, you must: reach N (does it actually sustain it?), exceed N (does it correctly back-pressure rather than overflow?), and sweep the range in between while measuring throughput. A design that silently drops a transaction at N+1 looks fine on a lightly loaded test and fails catastrophically in silicon under load.

Activity — size the outstanding requirement

A read path has a round-trip latency of 40 cycles. The bus can accept one new read address per cycle and return one data beat per cycle. How many reads must the manager keep outstanding to keep the bus 100% utilised, and what happens to utilisation if it can only keep 10 outstanding?

Use "outstanding needed ≈ latency × throughput." Throughput target is 1 transaction/cycle. For the second part, if you can only have 10 in flight but each takes 40 cycles, how many complete per 40-cycle window, and what fraction of the bus's 1-per-cycle capability is that?

Full utilisation: needed ≈ latency × throughput = 40 × 1 = 40 outstanding. With 40 in flight, by the time the 40th address is issued the 1st data is returning, and the pipe never stalls.

With only 10 outstanding: the manager issues 10 reads (10 cycles), then must wait — the first response doesn't arrive until cycle 40. So in each 40-cycle window only 10 transactions complete: utilisation ≈ 10/40 = 25%. The bus sits idle 75% of the time despite being capable of much more. This is precisely the kind of performance bug that outstanding-depth testing (and throughput coverage) is meant to catch.

What does increasing outstanding capability primarily improve?

Outstanding overlaps the latencies of many transactions, so more complete per unit time — that's throughput. The per-transaction latency is unchanged; clock frequency and burst length are unrelated knobs.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Derive your outstanding-depth test targets from the design's stated latency and throughput, not a round number.

Why: the design's buffers are sized for latency×throughput; testing to that number (and beyond) is what actually stresses them.

✗ Common mistake

Measuring only "did data match" and never measuring throughput/utilisation.

Why it hurts: a design can return correct data while silently starving the bus to 25% utilisation — a real performance bug invisible to a pure data scoreboard.

✓ Good practice

Include a low-outstanding case on purpose to confirm the design degrades gracefully, not just the high case.

Why: some bugs only appear when the pipe is shallow (e.g. a credit counter that underflows at low occupancy).

✗ Common mistake

Assuming "more outstanding is always faster" and never testing the saturation point.

Why it hurts: beyond latency×throughput, extra outstanding adds no speed but does stress buffers — and that's exactly where overflow bugs hide.

MODULE 3

Depth, IDs & the Ordering Rules

Learning objectives

  • Define total outstanding depth and per-ID outstanding depth.
  • Explain how AxID groups outstanding transactions into ordered streams.
  • State the same-ID / different-ID ordering rules precisely.
  • Understand separate read and write outstanding budgets.
Analogy — numbered deli tickets, multiple counters. At a deli, customers take a ticket number and the counter serves them. If everyone shares one number series at one counter, they're served strictly in order. But a big deli has several counters, each with its own number series — counter "5" serves its 5-tickets in order, counter "9" serves its 9-tickets in order, but counter 9 might finish before counter 5. The ticket-series is the AxID; same series = ordered, different series = independent.

Two kinds of depth

"How many can be outstanding" actually has two answers, and a real design specifies both:

LimitMeaningSet by
Total outstanding depthMax transactions in flight across all IDs at once.Size of the design's central tracking buffer.
Per-ID outstanding depthMax transactions in flight that share a single ID.Depth of that ID's reorder/ordering queue.

A design might allow 32 total outstanding but only 8 per ID. Both limits must be respected by stimulus and both are targets for "reach the limit, then exceed it" testing.

How AxID groups outstanding transactions

Every transaction carries an ID (ARID/AWID). Among the transactions currently outstanding, those sharing an ID form an ordered stream; the design must return their responses in issue order. Transactions with different IDs are independent and may complete in any relative order. The ID is what lets the manager match each returning response (RID/BID) to the request that spawned it.

The ordering rules, stated precisely

Rule 1 (same ID → ordered): outstanding transactions with the same AxID, in the same direction, must complete in the order their addresses were accepted.

Rule 2 (different ID → unordered): outstanding transactions with different AxIDs have no completion-order guarantee relative to each other.

Rule 3 (reads and writes are separate): read outstanding and write outstanding are tracked independently, on independent channels, with independent budgets.

Why this is the crux of both driver and scoreboard

These three rules are the contract your verification must both exercise and enforce. The driver must be able to produce many outstanding transactions spread across several IDs (to create the interesting interleavings). The scoreboard must accept any legal interleaving while flagging the one illegal thing: a within-ID reordering. Everything in Modules 4–7 is machinery for those two jobs.

The single most common mistake in this whole topic: building a scoreboard that assumes responses come back in issue order. The moment two different-ID transactions are outstanding and the design legally reorders them, that scoreboard reports a false failure. We design around this explicitly in Module 6.

Activity — legal or illegal completion order?

Outstanding reads, issued in this order: A(ID=3), B(ID=7), C(ID=3), D(ID=7), E(ID=3). They complete in this order: B, A, D, C, E. Is this legal? If not, identify the first violation.

Separate the completion sequence into two per-ID subsequences. For ID=3, what was the issue order and what was the completion order? Same for ID=7. A violation is any ID whose completion order differs from its issue order.

Split by ID:

  • ID=3 issued: A, C, E. In the completion sequence (B, A, D, C, E) the ID=3 items appear as A, C, E — matches issue order. ✓
  • ID=7 issued: B, D. In the completion sequence they appear as B, D — matches issue order. ✓

Legal. The interleaving B, A, D, C, E mixes the two streams freely (allowed), but each ID's internal order is preserved. Contrast: if completion were B, A, D, E, C, then ID=3 would complete A, E, C — E before C violates issue order (C was issued before E), so that would be illegal. Your scoreboard must accept the first and reject the second.

Two outstanding reads have different ARIDs. What does AXI guarantee about their completion order?

Different IDs carry no ordering guarantee — that freedom is what enables out-of-order completion and latency hiding. Only same-ID transactions must preserve issue order.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Constrain stimulus to respect both total and per-ID outstanding limits, then deliberately push one past its limit in dedicated tests.

Why: exceeding a limit unintentionally generates illegal traffic (false fails); never exceeding it leaves the back-pressure/overflow path untested.

✗ Common mistake

Tracking only a single global outstanding count and ignoring per-ID depth.

Why it hurts: a per-ID queue can overflow while the global count is well under its limit; a global-only check misses it entirely.

✓ Good practice

Use several distinct IDs in stimulus so the design's reorder logic is actually exercised.

Why: with one ID everything is in-order and the entire out-of-order datapath — the riskiest logic — is never touched.

✗ Common mistake

Sharing one outstanding budget across reads and writes in the model.

Why it hurts: reads and writes have independent channels and budgets; a merged model both mis-limits stimulus and mis-checks completion.

MODULE 4

How a Driver Issues Outstanding Transactions

Learning objectives

  • See why a naive blocking driver can never create outstanding transactions.
  • Understand the decoupling of address-phase from data/response-phase.
  • Write a pipelined UVM driver using fork...join_none and threads.
  • Handle the sequencer item-flow correctly when pipelining.
Analogy — a waiter who won't leave your table. A blocking driver is a waiter who takes your order, walks to the kitchen, waits for the food, brings it back, and only then takes the next table's order. A pipelined driver is a good waiter: take an order, fire it to the kitchen, immediately take the next table's order, and deliver dishes as they come up. Same kitchen speed, far more tables served.

The naive blocking driver — and why it fails the goal

The textbook first driver does one transaction end-to-end before fetching the next item. It is correct, but it can never have more than one transaction outstanding — it structurally serialises everything.

// BLOCKING driver — simple but only ever 1 outstanding. NOT what we want.
class axi_blocking_driver extends uvm_driver #(axi_seq_item);
  `uvm_component_utils(axi_blocking_driver)
  virtual axi_if vif;
  // ... build_phase gets vif from config_db ...

  task run_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    forever begin
      seq_item_port.get_next_item(req);   // fetch one item
      drive_address(req);                 // AR/AW handshake
      drive_data_and_wait_resp(req);      // W beats, then BLOCK until B/R completes
      seq_item_port.item_done();          // only now fetch the next
    end
  endtask
endclass

The problem is the order of operations: the driver waits for the response before calling item_done() and fetching the next item. The address channel sits idle during the entire response latency. To create outstanding transactions we must break that coupling.

The key idea: separate the address thread from the response thread

A pipelined driver runs (at least) two concurrent activities:

  • An address/issue thread that keeps pulling items and pushing their address phases onto the bus as fast as the DUT will accept them.
  • A response thread that independently collects completions as they return and retires the matching items.

Because these run in parallel, many addresses can be accepted while their responses are still pending — which is exactly "outstanding."

The pipelined UVM driver

Here is the core. The run_phase forks two persistent threads. The issue thread accepts items and drives addresses back-to-back; the response thread retires them as completions arrive.

class axi_pipelined_driver extends uvm_driver #(axi_seq_item);
  `uvm_component_utils(axi_pipelined_driver)
  virtual axi_if vif;

  // Per-ID queues of items whose address was issued but response not yet seen.
  // Indexed by ID; each entry is a FIFO of items in issue order for that ID.
  axi_seq_item outstanding[int][$];

  task run_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    // Two persistent, concurrent threads — this is what creates "outstanding".
    fork
      issue_thread();        // drives addresses as fast as the DUT accepts
      response_thread();     // collects responses, retires items
    join_none
  endtask

  // ---- Issue thread: never blocks on the response ----
  task issue_thread();
    forever begin
      seq_item_port.get_next_item(req);     // fetch next stimulus item
      drive_addr_phase(req);                // AW/AR handshake (blocks only until ACCEPTED)
      if (req.is_write) drive_write_data(req); // stream W beats (no wait for B)
      outstanding[req.id].push_back(req);   // record it as in-flight, in issue order
      seq_item_port.item_done();            // IMPORTANT: retire from sequencer NOW,
                                            // so the next item can be fetched immediately
    end
  endtask

  // ---- Response thread: independent of issue ----
  task response_thread();
    forever begin
      axi_resp r;
      collect_response(r);                  // waits for next B or R completion on the bus
      // Same-ID order guarantees the head of this ID's queue is the match.
      axi_seq_item done = outstanding[r.id].pop_front();
      done.resp = r.resp;                   // annotate result for analysis
      // (analysis export to scoreboard happens from the monitor, not here)
    end
  endtask
endclass
The one line that matters most: seq_item_port.item_done() is called right after the address (and write data) are accepted, not after the response returns. That single change — moving item_done() earlier — is what frees the sequencer to deliver the next item while the previous response is still pending, creating outstanding transactions. In the blocking driver it came after the response; here it comes before.

A subtlety: get_next_item vs item_done pairing

UVM allows only one get_next_item outstanding on the sequencer port at a time — you must call item_done() before the next get_next_item(). The pipelined driver honours this: the issue thread does get → drive-address → item_done() in a tight loop, and hands off response-tracking to the separate thread via the outstanding table. If you tried to defer item_done() until the response, you'd serialise again (and risk a sequencer protocol error).

Activity — find the pipelining bug

A junior engineer writes a "pipelined" driver but it still only ever shows one outstanding transaction in simulation. Their issue thread looks like this. What's wrong?

task issue_thread();
  forever begin
    seq_item_port.get_next_item(req);
    drive_addr_phase(req);
    wait_for_response(req);      // <-- waits here
    seq_item_port.item_done();
  end
endtask
Compare the position of the response-wait to the blocking driver from earlier. Even though the code is "in a thread," what does the issue loop do between accepting the address and fetching the next item?

The issue thread calls wait_for_response(req) before item_done(). That reintroduces the exact blocking behaviour: the loop cannot fetch the next item until the current response returns, so only one transaction is ever in flight. Forking two threads is pointless if the issue thread still blocks on the response.

Fix: remove the response wait from the issue loop entirely. Record the item in the outstanding table after the address is accepted, call item_done() immediately, and let the separate response thread handle completions. The response handling must live on the other side of the fork, not inline in the issue path.

In a pipelined driver, when should item_done() be called to enable outstanding transactions?

Calling item_done() right after acceptance frees the sequencer to deliver the next item while the response is still pending — that overlap is what creates outstanding transactions. Waiting for the response serialises everything back to one-at-a-time.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Separate the address-issue path from the response-collection path into independent forked threads.

Why: it's the only structure that lets addresses keep flowing while responses are pending — i.e. that actually produces outstanding transactions.

✗ Common mistake

Forking threads but still waiting for the response inside the issue loop.

Why it hurts: the wait re-serialises issue; you get the complexity of threads with none of the concurrency. Classic "looks pipelined, behaves blocking" bug.

✓ Good practice

Record each issued item in a per-ID outstanding structure the instant its address is accepted.

Why: the response thread needs an ordered, per-ID record to match completions against; same-ID ordering makes the head of the queue the guaranteed match.

✗ Common mistake

Deferring item_done() to the response, violating the one-get_next_item-at-a-time rule.

Why it hurts: it both serialises the driver and can trigger a UVM sequencer protocol error, which is confusing to debug.

MODULE 5

Tracking Outstanding & Handling Back-pressure

Learning objectives

  • Cap the driver's outstanding depth with a semaphore.
  • Enforce per-ID depth limits alongside the total limit.
  • Throttle issue cleanly when the DUT applies back-pressure.
  • Randomise outstanding depth to explore the full range of load.
Analogy — a kitchen with a ticket rail. A kitchen can only have so many open tickets on the rail before the cooks are overwhelmed. The expediter stops seating new orders when the rail is full and resumes as tickets are completed. The semaphore is that ticket rail: a hard cap on how many orders can be in flight, automatically pausing intake when full.

Why the driver needs a cap

A pipelined driver left unchecked will issue addresses as fast as the DUT accepts them — which is correct up to a point. But you usually want to (a) respect the DUT's stated outstanding limit, (b) deliberately control the depth to hit specific coverage points, and (c) avoid your own testbench data structures growing without bound. A counting semaphore gives all three.

Capping total outstanding with a semaphore

A SystemVerilog semaphore holds keys. Initialise it with N keys (the max outstanding). The issue thread must get a key before issuing; the response thread puts a key back when a transaction completes. When no keys remain, the issue thread blocks automatically — clean back-pressure with no manual polling.

class axi_throttled_driver extends uvm_driver #(axi_seq_item);
  `uvm_component_utils(axi_throttled_driver)
  virtual axi_if vif;

  int max_outstanding = 16;          // total cap (from DUT spec / config)
  semaphore slots;                       // one key == one allowed in-flight txn
  axi_seq_item outstanding[int][$];     // per-ID issue-order queues

  function void build_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    slots = new(max_outstanding);          // start with N keys
  endfunction

  task run_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    fork issue_thread(); response_thread(); join_none
  endtask

  task issue_thread();
    forever begin
      seq_item_port.get_next_item(req);
      slots.get(1);                       // BLOCKS here if N already outstanding
      drive_addr_phase(req);
      if (req.is_write) drive_write_data(req);
      outstanding[req.id].push_back(req);
      seq_item_port.item_done();
    end
  endtask

  task response_thread();
    forever begin
      axi_resp r;
      collect_response(r);
      void'(outstanding[r.id].pop_front()); // retire matching item (same-ID = head)
      slots.put(1);                       // return a key → unblocks issue_thread
    end
  endtask
endclass

Enforcing per-ID depth as well

The total cap alone doesn't stop one ID from hogging all slots, which may exceed a per-ID limit the DUT enforces. Add a per-ID semaphore (or counter) keyed by ID:

int max_per_id = 8;
  semaphore id_slots[int];               // one semaphore per ID, lazily created

  task issue_thread();
    forever begin
      seq_item_port.get_next_item(req);
      if (!id_slots.exists(req.id))
        id_slots[req.id] = new(max_per_id); // create on first use
      slots.get(1);                       // total cap
      id_slots[req.id].get(1);            // per-ID cap (both must be satisfied)
      drive_addr_phase(req);
      if (req.is_write) drive_write_data(req);
      outstanding[req.id].push_back(req);
      seq_item_port.item_done();
    end
  endtask
  // response_thread mirrors: id_slots[r.id].put(1); slots.put(1);
Watch the ordering of the two get() calls. Always acquire in a consistent order (e.g. total then per-ID) across all threads. Acquiring two resources in different orders in different code paths is a textbook recipe for a testbench deadlock — the same hazard the DUT itself must avoid.

Reacting to DUT back-pressure on the address channel

Separately from your own cap, the DUT can refuse new addresses by holding AWREADY/ARREADY low. The drive_addr_phase task naturally handles this: it asserts VALID and waits for READY, holding the payload stable. While it waits, no new item is fetched — so DUT back-pressure also throttles issue, for free. The two mechanisms compose: your semaphore caps the count; the DUT's READY caps the rate.

Randomising the depth to explore load

A fixed cap tests one operating point. To explore the range, randomise the effective cap per phase or inject random gaps in the issue thread, so the design sees shallow, medium, and saturated outstanding over a run:

// Vary load: occasionally idle the issue thread to create depth variation
if (!std::randomize(gap) with { gap dist {0 := 70, [1:5] := 30}; })
  `uvm_fatal("RAND","gap")
repeat(gap) @(posedge vif.clk);   // 70% no gap (push depth up), 30% small pause

Activity — diagnose the stuck driver

A driver uses a total semaphore initialised to 16 and a per-ID semaphore (max 8 each). After running a while with heavy single-ID traffic, the driver hangs — no new transactions issue, but the DUT is idle and accepting. The response thread calls only slots.put(1) on completion. What's the bug?

Count keys. The issue thread does TWO get() calls (total and per-ID). How many put() calls does the response thread do? With heavy single-ID traffic, which semaphore runs out of keys and never gets them back?

The response thread returns a key to the total semaphore but never to the per-ID semaphore (id_slots[r.id].put(1) is missing). With heavy single-ID traffic, that ID's per-ID semaphore drains to zero after 8 transactions and is never replenished, so the issue thread blocks forever on id_slots[req.id].get(1) — even though total slots and the DUT are free.

Fix: the response thread must mirror every acquire with a matching release: id_slots[r.id].put(1); slots.put(1);. Every get() needs exactly one corresponding put(), or the pool leaks keys and eventually deadlocks. This is the most common semaphore bug in throttled drivers.

What is the cleanest way for a pipelined driver to cap its outstanding depth at N?

A counting semaphore caps depth automatically: the issue thread blocks when keys run out and resumes when completions return them. Polling is wasteful and error-prone, fixed delays don't track actual completions, and limiting total items caps the run length, not the in-flight depth.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Pair every semaphore get() with exactly one put() on the completion path, for both total and per-ID pools.

Why: a leaked key permanently lowers the effective cap and eventually deadlocks the driver — a hang that's painful to trace.

✗ Common mistake

Returning the total key but forgetting the per-ID key (or vice versa).

Why it hurts: one pool slowly drains to zero and the driver wedges, often only under sustained single-ID load — so it passes light tests and hangs in regression.

✓ Good practice

Acquire multiple resources in a single consistent order everywhere.

Why: inconsistent acquire order between threads is a classic deadlock; keeping it uniform makes the testbench race-free by construction.

✗ Common mistake

Driving at a single fixed outstanding depth for the whole run.

Why it hurts: you test one occupancy point. Bugs at shallow depth (counter underflow) and at saturation (overflow/back-pressure) both go unseen. Randomise the depth.

MODULE 6

Scoreboard Architecture for Outstanding Transactions

Learning objectives

  • See why a single in-order FIFO scoreboard fails with outstanding transactions.
  • Design the per-ID expected-queue model.
  • Match returning responses to requests using ID.
  • Build the scoreboard skeleton with UVM analysis ports.
Analogy — a coat check with numbered tickets. A coat check doesn't assume coats are collected in the order they were handed in. It gives each coat a numbered ticket and files it. When you return your ticket, it finds your coat by number, regardless of who arrived before or after you. The scoreboard is the coat check; the AxID is the ticket number; the per-ID queues are the numbered racks.

How a scoreboard works, in one sentence

A scoreboard receives a copy of every request (from a monitor on the address side) and every response (from a monitor on the data/response side), computes what the response should be, and checks the actual response against the expectation. With outstanding transactions, the only hard part is matching each response to the right request.

Why the naive single-FIFO scoreboard fails

The textbook scoreboard pushes each expected result into one queue as requests arrive, and pops from the front as responses arrive — assuming responses come back in issue order. The instant two different-ID transactions are outstanding and the DUT legally reorders them, the front of the FIFO is the wrong transaction, and the scoreboard reports a mismatch that is not a bug.

The per-ID expected-queue model

The fix follows directly from the ordering rules (Module 3): keep one expected-queue per ID. On a request, enqueue the expected result into that ID's queue. On a response, use the response's ID to pick the queue, and compare against its head (same-ID ordering guarantees the oldest outstanding item of that ID is the match). This single data structure encodes both ordering rules: strict order within a queue, independence across queues.

The scoreboard skeleton with analysis ports

In UVM, monitors broadcast observed transactions through uvm_analysis_port. The scoreboard receives them via uvm_analysis_imp. We use two — one for requests, one for responses — with the macro `uvm_analysis_imp_decl to give each its own write method.

// Declare two distinct analysis imp suffixes: _req and _rsp
`uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_req)
`uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_rsp)

class axi_scoreboard extends uvm_scoreboard;
  `uvm_component_utils(axi_scoreboard)

  uvm_analysis_imp_req #(axi_seq_item, axi_scoreboard) req_imp;
  uvm_analysis_imp_rsp #(axi_seq_item, axi_scoreboard) rsp_imp;

  // THE key data structure: per-ID queues of expected results, in issue order.
  axi_seq_item expected[int][$];   // expected[id] is a FIFO for that ID

  // A reference memory model so we can predict read data and apply writes.
  bit [7:0] mem [bit[31:0]];   // sparse byte-addressable memory

  int unsigned matches, mismatches;

  function void build_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    req_imp = new("req_imp", this);
    rsp_imp = new("rsp_imp", this);
  endfunction

  // Called by the address-side monitor for every accepted request.
  function void write_req(axi_seq_item t);
    axi_seq_item exp = predict(t);        // compute expected result from the ref model
    expected[t.id].push_back(exp);        // enqueue in issue order, per ID
  endfunction

  // Called by the response-side monitor for every completed response.
  function void write_rsp(axi_seq_item actual);
    if (expected[actual.id].size() == 0) begin
      `uvm_error("SB", $sformatf("Response for ID %0d with no outstanding request!", actual.id))
      return;
    end
    axi_seq_item exp = expected[actual.id].pop_front(); // same-ID order ⇒ head is the match
    compare(exp, actual);
  endfunction
endclass

predict() and compare() — the reference model

The scoreboard's correctness rests on a faithful reference model. predict() turns a request into the expected response; for a write it applies the data to the reference memory (honouring byte strobes), for a read it gathers the expected bytes. compare() checks the actual response against it.

function axi_seq_item predict(axi_seq_item t);
  axi_seq_item exp = axi_seq_item::type_id::create("exp");
  exp.id = t.id; exp.is_write = t.is_write;
  foreach (t.beat_addr[i]) begin            // per-beat addresses (from burst calc)
    if (t.is_write) begin
      for (int b=0; b<t.bytes_per_beat; b++)
        if (t.wstrb[i][b])               // only strobed bytes are written
          mem[t.beat_addr[i]+b] = t.wdata[i][8*b +: 8];
      exp.resp = OKAY;                   // expected B response
    end else begin
      for (int b=0; b<t.bytes_per_beat; b++)
        exp.rdata[i][8*b +: 8] = mem.exists(t.beat_addr[i]+b)
                                       ? mem[t.beat_addr[i]+b] : 8'h00;
      exp.resp = OKAY;                   // per-beat RRESP
    end
  end
  return exp;
endfunction

function void compare(axi_seq_item exp, axi_seq_item act);
  if (act.is_write) begin
    if (act.resp !== exp.resp)
      `uvm_error("SB", $sformatf("ID %0d write resp exp %s got %s", act.id, exp.resp.name, act.resp.name))
    else matches++;
  end else begin
    foreach (exp.rdata[i])
      if (act.rdata[i] !== exp.rdata[i]) begin
        `uvm_error("SB", $sformatf("ID %0d beat %0d data exp %h got %h", act.id, i, exp.rdata[i], act.rdata[i]))
        mismatches++; return;
      end
    matches++;
  end
endfunction
Where requests and responses come from: the scoreboard does not snoop the driver. Two independent monitors observe the bus — one watches the address channels and emits the request to write_req; the other watches the data/response channels and emits the completion to write_rsp. Keeping the scoreboard fed by monitors (not the driver) means it verifies what actually happened on the wires, not what the driver intended.

Activity — choose the data structure

You must store expected results for up to 32 outstanding transactions spread across up to 4 distinct IDs, where responses can return out of order across IDs but in order within an ID. Someone proposes a single axi_seq_item expected[$] queue and "search it for a matching ID on each response." Why is the per-ID queue (expected[int][$]) better, both for correctness and for performance?

Think about how you find the right expected item on a response. With one queue you must search; which matching item do you pick if an ID has several entries? With per-ID queues, where is the guaranteed match, and how long does finding it take?

Correctness: with per-ID queues the match for a response is always the head of that ID's queue — guaranteed by same-ID ordering. With a single searched queue you must decide which matching-ID entry to pick; pick the wrong one (e.g. not the oldest) and you mis-compare. The per-ID structure bakes the ordering rule into the data structure so you can't get it wrong.

Performance: per-ID is an O(1) pop_front on a small queue. The single-queue search is O(N) per response, and with deep outstanding in long regressions that overhead is real. The per-ID map also makes per-ID depth tracking and coverage trivial. It is both safer and faster.

Why does a single in-order FIFO scoreboard produce false mismatches with outstanding transactions?

Different-ID transactions may complete in any order. A single FIFO assumes issue-order completion, so when a later-issued different-ID response returns first, the FIFO front is the wrong item and the compare fails — a testbench bug, not an RTL bug. Per-ID queues fix it.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Use a per-ID expected-queue map; pop the head of the response's ID queue to find the match.

Why: it encodes both ordering rules in the data structure, is O(1) per response, and makes per-ID depth tracking free.

✗ Common mistake

Feeding the scoreboard from the driver instead of from monitors.

Why it hurts: you'd be checking what the driver intended, not what reached the wires — bugs in the driver or DUT pin-level behaviour become invisible.

✓ Good practice

Flag a response that arrives with an empty expected-queue for its ID as an error.

Why: an unexpected/extra response (or a duplicated one) is a real protocol bug; silently ignoring it hides DUT errors.

✗ Common mistake

Modelling read data by reading the DUT's memory backdoor instead of a reference model.

Why it hurts: backdoor reads can mask the very datapath bug you're testing; an independent reference model is what makes the check meaningful.

MODULE 7

Out-of-Order Completion in the Scoreboard

Learning objectives

  • Confirm that per-ID queues accept all legal reorderings automatically.
  • Detect the one illegal case: within-ID reordering.
  • Add per-transaction timeouts to catch dropped (never-returning) responses.
  • Handle read-data interleaving on the R channel.
Analogy — a relay race with multiple lanes. Each lane (ID) must pass its baton in strict runner order — runner 2 can't finish before runner 1 in the same lane. But lane 3 can finish its whole relay before lane 1 finishes its first leg. The referee (scoreboard) only disqualifies a lane that passes its baton out of order within the lane; cross-lane finishing order is never a foul.

Legal reordering is already handled — by construction

Here's the elegant part: the per-ID queue model from Module 6 needs no extra code to accept out-of-order completion across IDs. Because each response is routed to its own ID's queue, responses from different IDs arriving in any order each find the correct head. The scoreboard simply doesn't care about the relative order of different-ID responses — which is exactly the protocol rule.

The structure is the check. Routing by ID + comparing against the head of that ID's queue means: across IDs → any order works (different queues); within an ID → order is enforced (FIFO discipline). The two ordering rules fall out of the data structure for free.

Detecting illegal within-ID reordering

So what catches a bug? If the DUT returns two same-ID responses out of order, the data at the head of that ID's queue won't match the returned data — the compare fails. That's the detection. But the failure message ("data mismatch") doesn't scream "ordering violation," so it's worth adding an explicit check that makes the diagnosis obvious. Tag each expected item with a per-ID sequence number and verify responses arrive in increasing sequence within the ID:

int issue_seq[int];        // next issue sequence number, per ID
int expect_seq[int];       // next expected completion sequence, per ID

function void write_req(axi_seq_item t);
  t.seq_no = issue_seq[t.id]++;     // stamp issue order within this ID
  axi_seq_item exp = predict(t);
  exp.seq_no = t.seq_no;
  expected[t.id].push_back(exp);
endfunction

function void write_rsp(axi_seq_item actual);
  if (expected[actual.id].size() == 0) begin
    `uvm_error("SB", $sformatf("Unexpected response, ID %0d", actual.id)) return;
  end
  axi_seq_item exp = expected[actual.id].pop_front();
  // Explicit ordering guard: the head MUST be the next expected sequence.
  if (exp.seq_no !== expect_seq[actual.id]) begin
    `uvm_error("SB_ORDER", $sformatf(
       "ID %0d completed out of order: expected seq %0d, head seq %0d",
       actual.id, expect_seq[actual.id], exp.seq_no))
  end
  expect_seq[actual.id]++;
  compare(exp, actual);
endfunction

Now a within-ID ordering violation produces a clear SB_ORDER error naming the IDs and sequence numbers — far easier to triage than a bare data mismatch.

Timeouts — catching the response that never comes

The most dangerous bug is a dropped transaction: the DUT accepts a request and never returns its response. Nothing mismatches — the expected item just sits in its queue forever, and a naive scoreboard reports success at end-of-test. You must actively hunt this with a timeout.

// Stamp each expected item with the time it was issued; a watchdog scans for staleness.
function void write_req(axi_seq_item t);
  t.issue_time = $time;
  // ... seq stamping + predict + push_back as before ...
endfunction

task run_phase(uvm_phase phase);
  forever begin
    #(1000);                                // check periodically
    foreach (expected[id])
      if (expected[id].size() > 0) begin
        axi_seq_item head = expected[id][0];
        if ($time - head.issue_time > TIMEOUT_NS)
          `uvm_error("SB_TIMEOUT", $sformatf(
            "ID %0d txn (seq %0d) outstanding > %0d ns — response dropped?",
            id, head.seq_no, TIMEOUT_NS))
      end
  end
endtask

// Also verify at end-of-test that NOTHING is left outstanding.
function void check_phase(uvm_phase phase);
  foreach (expected[id])
    if (expected[id].size() != 0)
      `uvm_error("SB_LEFTOVER", $sformatf("ID %0d has %0d unmatched requests at end of test",
                 id, expected[id].size()))
endfunction
Two safety nets, both required. The periodic timeout catches a hang during the run (so the regression fails fast with a useful message). The check_phase leftover-scan catches anything still outstanding at the end. Without both, a dropped response can masquerade as a passing test — the most insidious verification escape there is.

Read-data interleaving

One more wrinkle for reads: AXI allows R-channel beats of different IDs to interleave (the RID on each beat says which transaction it belongs to). Your response monitor must reassemble beats per RID into complete transactions before handing them to write_rsp. (Note: AXI4 removed write-data interleaving, so the W side is contiguous; only reads need per-RID beat reassembly.)

Activity — will this scoreboard catch the bug?

A DUT has a bug: under load, it occasionally drops a single read response entirely (accepts the AR, never returns the R). A reviewer says "our per-ID-queue scoreboard with data comparison will catch it." Are they right? What exactly is needed to catch this class of bug?

If a response never arrives, is write_rsp ever called for it? Does the compare ever run? What happens to the expected item — and when, if ever, would a pure data-comparison scoreboard notice it's still sitting there?

Not by data comparison alone. A dropped response means write_rsp is never called for that transaction, so compare() never runs — there is no mismatch to report. The expected item simply stays in its ID's queue. A scoreboard that only compares on response will report a clean pass.

What catches it: the timeout watchdog (flags the stale head of the queue during the run) and/or the check_phase leftover-scan (flags the unmatched item at end-of-test). This is why those two mechanisms aren't optional extras — they catch an entire class of bugs that data comparison structurally cannot. Add an end-to-end transaction-count check (issued vs completed per ID) for belt-and-braces.

With per-ID queues, what extra logic is needed to accept legal out-of-order completion across different IDs?

Because each response is matched against the head of its own ID's queue, different-ID responses arriving in any order each find the right expected item with no extra logic. The data structure itself encodes "any order across IDs, strict order within an ID."

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Add an explicit per-ID sequence guard so within-ID reordering produces a clear, named error.

Why: relying on a data mismatch to flag an ordering bug gives a misleading message; an SB_ORDER error names the exact IDs and sequence numbers.

✗ Common mistake

Trusting a data-comparison scoreboard to catch dropped responses.

Why it hurts: a never-returning response triggers no compare and looks like a pass — the worst kind of silent escape. You need timeouts and a leftover-scan.

✓ Good practice

Check at check_phase that every per-ID queue is empty and issued counts equal completed counts.

Why: it's the definitive end-of-test proof that nothing was lost or duplicated, independent of data values.

✗ Common mistake

Forgetting to reassemble interleaved read beats per RID before comparing.

Why it hurts: interleaved R beats from different IDs will be stitched into the wrong transactions, producing false data mismatches that look like DUT bugs.

MODULE 8

Coverage & Corner Cases

Learning objectives

  • Build a functional coverage model specific to outstanding behaviour.
  • Cover outstanding depth, per-ID depth, and out-of-order "distance."
  • Enumerate the "try to break it" corners for outstanding transactions.
  • Understand why coverage, not passing tests, defines completeness here.
Analogy — a checklist for a test pilot. A plane that lands safely once hasn't been tested — it's been flown. Real testing is a checklist of regimes: max load, stall, sharp turns, engine-out. Functional coverage is that checklist for the design: it records which regimes you actually flew, so "all green" means "we deliberately exercised every regime," not "nothing happened to break."

What to cover for outstanding transactions

The performance and ordering arguments tell you exactly what regimes matter. The coverage model should capture the occupancy of the system and the shape of the reordering, not just individual transactions.

class axi_outstanding_cov extends uvm_subscriber #(axi_event);
  `uvm_component_utils(axi_outstanding_cov)

  int cur_total;        // current total outstanding (updated on issue/complete)
  int cur_per_id;       // outstanding for the ID of the sampled event
  int ooo_distance;     // how far out of issue order this completion was
  bit [3:0] id;

  covergroup cg;
    // How deep did we actually go? Hitting the max bin is the key goal.
    depth_cp : coverpoint cur_total {
      bins empty   = {0};
      bins low     = {[1:4]};
      bins mid     = {[5:12]};
      bins near_max= {[13:15]};
      bins at_max  = {16};          // the cap — must be hit
    }
    per_id_cp : coverpoint cur_per_id {
      bins one = {1}; bins few = {[2:7]}; bins at_id_max = {8};
    }
    // Reorder shape: 0 = in order; larger = a response jumped ahead of N older ones.
    ooo_cp : coverpoint ooo_distance {
      bins inorder = {0};
      bins near    = {[1:3]};
      bins far     = {[4:31]};   // a much-later txn overtaking
    }
    id_cp : coverpoint id;
    // Crosses — bugs cluster at intersections, not single values.
    depth_x_ooo : cross depth_cp, ooo_cp;     // deep AND heavily reordered?
    id_x_depth  : cross id_cp, per_id_cp;     // did EACH id reach its per-id max?
  endgroup

  function void write(axi_event e);
    // update cur_total / cur_per_id / ooo_distance / id from e, then:
    cg.sample();
  endfunction
endclass
The cross is the point. depth_x_ooo is the coverage bin that matters most: "reached max outstanding and saw a far reorder" is precisely the high-stress state where buffer and reorder logic interact. Covering depth alone or reorder alone can read 100% while that interaction was never hit — and that interaction is where the nastiest bugs live.

The "try to break it" corner cases

Beyond random coverage, write directed tests for the corners that random rarely lands on cleanly:

Corner caseWhat it stresses / why it bites
Reach exactly the max outstanding, then issue one moreThe accept/back-pressure boundary — does the DUT correctly stall vs overflow its buffer?

Why coverage defines "done" here

For outstanding transactions, "all tests pass" is especially weak: a shallow random run can pass forever while never reaching max depth or a far reorder. Completion is when the coverage model — especially the depth and depth×ooo crosses — is closed. That's your evidence that the high-stress states the design was built to survive were actually visited.

Activity — design the highest-value coverage cross

Your DUT supports 16 total / 8 per-ID outstanding, with out-of-order completion across 4 IDs. You can add one more coverage cross to your model. Which would you add to most increase confidence, and why — what bug would it be designed to expose?

The riskiest states combine high occupancy with heavy reordering. You already have depth×ooo. What about whether the design holds up when it's simultaneously near max total depth AND a specific ID is at its per-ID max — i.e. one ID dominating a full buffer?

A strong choice: cross (cur_total at/near max) × (cur_per_id at id_max) × (ooo far) — the state where the buffer is nearly full, a single ID is holding its maximum share, and a far reorder occurs.

Bug it targets: ID-tracking/allocation logic that works when load is spread across IDs but mismanages pointers when one ID monopolises a near-full buffer and responses come back badly reordered. That's a realistic corner where a reorder buffer's per-ID free-list or CAM can mis-allocate — and it's almost impossible to hit by accident, so an explicit cross (plus a directed test to fill the bin) is the only way to prove you exercised it. Covering total depth, per-ID depth, and ooo independently could all read 100% while this triple-intersection was never reached.

Why is functional coverage (not just passing tests) the right completion criterion for outstanding-transaction verification?

Tests passing only means the states you happened to reach were clean. The dangerous states (max depth, far reorder, their cross) may never have occurred. Functional coverage measures which of those regimes were actually visited — that's what "done" means here. It complements, not replaces, the scoreboard.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Make "reached max outstanding" and "depth × out-of-order distance" explicit coverage bins.

Why: these are the high-stress regimes the design was sized for; if you don't measure them you can't claim to have verified them.

✗ Common mistake

Covering depth and reorder independently and declaring victory at 100% each.

Why it hurts: the interaction (deep AND reordered) can be entirely unexercised while both individual coverpoints read full — and that interaction is where bugs hide.

✓ Good practice

Pair the coverage model with directed corner tests (max+1, all-same-ID, reset-with-N-outstanding).

Why: random stimulus rarely lands cleanly on the exact boundary; directed tests guarantee the corner bins close.

✗ Common mistake

Never testing reset while transactions are outstanding.

Why it hurts: reset-with-in-flight-traffic is a top source of silicon bugs (stale pointers, stuck counters) and is trivially missed by steady-state random tests.

Analogy — the full customs operation. The throttled driver is the gate letting in a managed number of trucks (outstanding cap). The monitors are inspectors recording every truck in and every truck out. The scoreboard is the manifest office matching departures to arrivals by tracking number (ID). Coverage is the logbook proving every category of cargo and every load level was inspected. Together they prove nothing slipped through.

The complete environment

Wiring it together

class axi_env extends uvm_env;
  `uvm_component_utils(axi_env)
  axi_agent       agent;     // contains sequencer + throttled driver + 2 monitors
  axi_scoreboard  sb;
  axi_outstanding_cov cov;

  function void build_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    agent = axi_agent::type_id::create("agent", this);
    sb    = axi_scoreboard::type_id::create("sb", this);
    cov   = axi_outstanding_cov::type_id::create("cov", this);
  endfunction

  function void connect_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    // Address-side monitor → scoreboard request input
    agent.addr_mon.ap.connect(sb.req_imp);
    // Response-side monitor → scoreboard response input + coverage
    agent.resp_mon.ap.connect(sb.rsp_imp);
    agent.resp_mon.ap.connect(cov.analysis_export);
  endfunction
endclass

Debug walkthrough — "intermittent mismatch under load"

Symptom. A nightly regression fails ~1 run in 20 with SB data mismatches on ID 2. It only fails when the random seed produces heavy multi-ID traffic near max depth. Handshake (protocol) assertions are all silent. Reproducing with the logged seed is reliable.

Step 1 — trust the silence of the assertions. Protocol assertions passing means the bus behaviour is legal AXI (handshakes, RLAST, response legality all fine). So the fault is either a real data/ordering bug in the DUT or a testbench modelling error. Don't assume DUT yet.

Step 2 — read the failure's shape. Mismatch only on heavy multi-ID load near max depth points squarely at out-of-order handling. Single-ID and shallow runs pass — so the per-ID data is fine in isolation; the problem appears only when reordering across IDs is intense.

Step 3 — form hypotheses, cheapest first.

  1. TB: single-FIFO leak. Is the scoreboard truly per-ID, or did someone "optimise" it back to a shared queue? A shared queue mis-pairs exactly under heavy reorder. Check first — it's the most common cause and easy to confirm.
  2. TB: read-beat interleaving. If R beats of different IDs interleave and the monitor stitches them by arrival rather than by RID, it builds the wrong transaction → false mismatch on the busiest IDs.
  3. DUT: reorder buffer aliasing. A genuine bug where, near full occupancy, the DUT's per-ID tracking aliases two entries and returns ID 2's data for the wrong request.

Step 4 — localise with the sequence guard. Enable the SB_ORDER check (Module 7). If it fires before the data mismatch, the completion order itself is wrong → suspect DUT reorder logic (hypothesis 3) or monitor reassembly (hypothesis 2). If SB_ORDER stays silent but data still mismatches, the order is correct and the data is wrong → reference-model/strobe issue or a DUT datapath bug.

Step 5 — confirm with a minimal directed test. Recreate the seed's essential shape with a tiny directed test: 4 IDs, fill to max depth, complete newest-first. If it reproduces, you have a 20-line repro instead of a 10-million-cycle regression — and the per-ID queue dump at the mismatch cycle tells you immediately whether the head was the wrong item (TB) or the right item with wrong data (DUT).

The discipline: silent protocol assertions → suspect TB or data/ordering, not handshakes. Shape of failure (heavy reorder, near max) → narrows to the out-of-order path. Cheapest hypothesis first (is the scoreboard really per-ID?). Then a named ordering guard and a minimal repro to split TB-bug from DUT-bug. This sequence resolves the large majority of outstanding-transaction failures.

Activity — full root-cause reasoning

Using the debug walkthrough's method: a regression fails with SB_TIMEOUT on ID 0 only, only when writes and reads run at full depth simultaneously. No data mismatches, no SB_ORDER. What is your leading hypothesis and your next concrete step?

A timeout (not a mismatch) means a response never came back — a dropped transaction. It happens only when reads AND writes are both at full depth. What shared resource might reads and writes contend for that should be independent? Recall the rule that read and write outstanding budgets are separate.

Leading hypothesis: a dropped response caused by read/write budget interference. The timeout (not a mismatch) means a transaction for ID 0 was accepted but never completed. That it appears only when reads and writes are both saturated points at a shared resource that should be independent — e.g. a combined outstanding counter or a shared buffer slot pool in the DUT that lets write pressure starve a read response (or vice versa), violating the "reads and writes are separate budgets" rule.

Next concrete step: reproduce with the logged seed, then run two control experiments — (a) reads-only at full depth, (b) writes-only at full depth. If both pass and only the combination fails, that strongly confirms cross-budget interference in the DUT rather than a testbench bug. Then dump the DUT's outstanding counters at the timeout to see whether the read path's credits were consumed by write traffic. A minimal directed test (saturate writes, issue one read to ID 0, watch for its response) gives a tiny repro for the designer.

In the debug method, what does it tell you when protocol (handshake) assertions are silent but the scoreboard reports a mismatch?

Silent protocol assertions mean the observed handshakes and ordering primitives are legal. A mismatch then points to the checking/modelling side (scoreboard assuming in-order, monitor mis-reassembling interleaved beats, reference-model/strobe error) or a functional DUT data/ordering bug — but not a handshake violation. That narrowing is the first move in efficient debug.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Feed the scoreboard and coverage from monitors on the bus, connected via analysis ports in connect_phase.

Why: it decouples checking from stimulus and verifies real pin-level behaviour; the same scoreboard then works with any driver or in passive (monitor-only) reuse.

✗ Common mistake

When debugging, assuming a scoreboard mismatch means a DUT bug.

Why it hurts: with outstanding traffic, the most common cause is a testbench that mishandles legal reordering. Rule out the TB first or you'll waste days blaming correct RTL.

✓ Good practice

Reduce a failing seed to a minimal directed repro before deep-diving.

Why: a 20-line repro turns an intractable 10M-cycle failure into something you can single-step; it also becomes a permanent regression test for the fixed bug.

✗ Common mistake

Shipping when tests pass but the depth and depth×ooo coverage bins are still open.

Why it hurts: open high-stress bins mean the regimes the design was built to survive were never exercised — passing tests there prove nothing.
