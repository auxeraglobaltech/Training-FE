MODULE 1

The DUT — Our 2×2×2 NoC

Learning objectives

  • Describe the exact topology we will verify: 2 AXI masters, 2 AXI slaves, 2 APB slaves.
  • List the four jobs the NoC performs on every transaction.
  • Read the address map that defines where each slave lives.
  • See the complete verification surface so every later module has a place.
Analogy — a small postal hub. Two people drop letters (the AXI masters). The hub reads each address and sorts it to one of four mailboxes (two AXI slaves, two APB slaves). When both people want the same mailbox at once, a clerk decides who goes first (arbitration). Some mailboxes speak a different language, so the hub translates (the AXI→APB bridge). The NoC is that hub: route, arbitrate, translate, and never lose a letter.

What a NoC is, in one paragraph

A Network-on-Chip (NoC) — here a compact interconnect — is the fabric that connects initiators (masters) to targets (slaves) so that any master can reach any slave it is allowed to, even when several masters compete at once. Our DUT is deliberately small enough to reason about completely, yet it contains every hard problem a large fabric has: routing, arbitration, protocol conversion, ordering, and deadlock avoidance. Master it here and the concepts scale up.

The complete verification surface

Naming the whole job up front keeps the rest of the course organised. To sign off this NoC you must prove:

  • Connectivity: every legal master→slave path works (M0→S0, M0→S1, M0→P0, M0→P1, and the same for M1) — a 2×4 routing matrix.
  • Decode correctness: in-range routes to the right slave; out-of-range returns DECERR; no overlaps or holes.
  • Arbitration: round-robin is fair, priority is honoured, no master starves, the combined scheme behaves per spec.
  • Protocol conversion: AXI bursts/responses correctly become APB transfers and back.
  • Concurrency: ordering and outstanding limits hold under simultaneous multi-master load; no deadlock.
  • Robustness: errors, reset under load, and saturation are handled cleanly.
Why this DUT is the perfect teaching vehicle: it is small enough that your scoreboard can model every slave's memory and every legal path exhaustively, yet it contains arbitration, conversion, and concurrency — the three things that make real interconnect verification hard. Nothing here is a toy; it is a real fabric in miniature.

Activity — map the routing matrix

List every legal master→target path in this DUT and state how many distinct connectivity paths your test plan must cover at minimum. Then name one path that does not exist and explain why.

There are 2 masters and 4 targets (S0, S1, P0, P1). Connectivity is master × target. Masters talk to targets — do masters talk to each other? Do slaves initiate?

Legal paths (2 × 4 = 8): M0→S0, M0→S1, M0→P0, M0→P1, M1→S0, M1→S1, M1→P0, M1→P1. Your connectivity test plan must cover all 8 (each with reads and writes where applicable).

A path that does not exist: M0→M1 (master to master). Masters are initiators only; they do not respond to transactions, so there is no master-to-master path. Likewise S0→S1 doesn't exist — slaves are targets, they never initiate. Recognising the legal surface keeps you from writing meaningless tests and tells you exactly how big the connectivity matrix is.

A master issues a transaction to address 0x3000_0000. What must the NoC do?

0x3000_0000 falls outside every slave's range in the address map, so it's unmapped. A correct NoC must return DECERR so the master can recover — never drop, hang, or guess a target. Probing unmapped addresses is a required test.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Derive a single machine-readable address map and feed it to stimulus, the decoder model, and the scoreboard from one source.

Why: three hand-copied maps drift apart; one source means a map change updates stimulus and checking together, eliminating a whole class of false fails.

✗ Common mistake

Verifying only the "obvious" paths (M0→S0) and assuming the rest are symmetric.

Why it hurts: routing and arbitration logic is per-port; M1→P1 can be broken while M0→S0 works. The full 2×4 matrix must be exercised.

✓ Good practice

Write the verification surface (connectivity, decode, arbitration, conversion, concurrency, robustness) into the test plan before coding.

Why: the test plan is what coverage closure is measured against; an explicit surface prevents "we never tested the APB error path" gaps.

✗ Common mistake

Treating the APB slaves as "just two more slaves" identical to the AXI ones.

Why it hurts: they sit behind a protocol bridge with completely different timing and rules; modelling them like AXI slaves produces wrong expectations and missed bridge bugs.

MODULE 2

Routing & Address Decoding

Learning objectives

  • Trace how a master's address is decoded to one of the four targets.
  • Understand decode errors and why the NoC must never hang on one.
  • See how two masters routing concurrently makes decode a per-port problem.
  • Write the decode reference model your scoreboard will reuse.
Analogy — the sorting clerk. Every letter has a postcode. The clerk glances at the first few digits and drops it into the right bin without reading the whole address. The NoC's decoder does the same: it looks at the upper address bits, picks the target, and forwards the transaction. A postcode that matches no bin goes to the "return to sender" tray — that's a decode error.

Decode = address in, target out

When a master presents a transaction, the NoC compares the address against each slave's range and selects the one target that owns it. Because the ranges in our map are aligned on neat power-of-two boundaries, the decoder only needs to examine the upper address bits — fast, cheap combinational logic.

The decode reference model

This function is the heart of routing and you will reuse it verbatim in the scoreboard (Module 8) to predict where every transaction should land. Build it once, from the single-source address map.

typedef enum {TGT_S0, TGT_S1, TGT_P0, TGT_P1, TGT_NONE} target_e;

// Decode an address to its target. TGT_NONE means a decode error (DECERR).
function automatic target_e decode(bit[31:0] addr);
  if      (addr inside {[32'h0000_0000:32'h0FFF_FFFF]}) return TGT_S0;
  else if (addr inside {[32'h1000_0000:32'h1FFF_FFFF]}) return TGT_S1;
  else if (addr inside {[32'h2000_0000:32'h2000_FFFF]}) return TGT_P0;
  else if (addr inside {[32'h2001_0000:32'h2001_FFFF]}) return TGT_P1;
  else return TGT_NONE;   // unmapped → DUT must return DECERR
endfunction

Decode errors — the NoC must never hang

An unmapped access is not an excuse for the fabric to lock up. The NoC must itself generate a clean DECERR response (on B for a write, on every R beat for a read) so the master recovers. The classic system bug: an unmapped access reaches no slave, no slave responds, and the NoC forgot to generate the error — so the master waits forever and the bus deadlocks. Your tests must deliberately fire unmapped addresses and confirm a prompt DECERR, not a hang.

The 4 KB boundary still applies. An AXI burst must not cross a 4 KB boundary, which guarantees a single burst targets a single slave. A burst engineered to straddle two slaves' ranges is illegal stimulus — your generator must not produce it, and a NoC checker should flag it if it appears. This keeps routing unambiguous: one transaction, one target.

Why decode is a per-port problem

Both masters decode independently and simultaneously. M0 might route to S1 in the same cycle M1 routes to P0 — fine, different targets, both proceed. But if both decode to S1 at once, decode has done its job (both correctly identified S1) and the conflict passes to arbitration (Modules 3–4). Keep the two stages separate in your mental model: decode answers "which target?", arbitration answers "who first?".

Activity — predict the routing for concurrent traffic

In one cycle: M0 issues to 0x2001_0040 and M1 issues to 0x0800_0000. For each, give the target and say whether the two transactions contend. Then: M0 issues to 0x1000_0000 and M1 to 0x1F00_0000 — do these contend?

Run each address through the decode ranges. Two transactions contend only if they decode to the same target. Different targets proceed in parallel; same target goes to arbitration.

First pair: 0x2001_0040 → P1 (in 0x2001_0000–0x2001_FFFF). 0x0800_0000 → S0 (in 0x0000_0000–0x0FFF_FFFF). Different targets → no contention; both proceed in parallel.

Second pair: 0x1000_0000 → S1 and 0x1F00_0000 → S1 (both in S1's range, even though the addresses differ). Same target → they contend, and arbitration decides which master accesses S1 first. Note: contention is about the target, not the exact address — two different addresses in the same slave still compete for that slave's port.

Decode determines "which target." What determines the order when two masters decode to the same target?

Decode just identifies the target; when two masters want the same target, arbitration (the subject of the next two modules) picks the order. Address value and master ID don't decide ordering, and a legal same-target conflict is never a decode error.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Reuse the exact same decode() function in stimulus, the scoreboard, and any coverage model.

Why: a single decode source guarantees the scoreboard predicts the same target the DUT should pick; two implementations drift and create false mismatches.

✗ Common mistake

Never testing unmapped addresses because "software won't generate them."

Why it hurts: stray/buggy software and security probes absolutely do generate them; an un-handled hole is a hang that escapes to silicon.

✓ Good practice

Test the exact boundary addresses of each range (base, base−1, end, end+1).

Why: off-by-one decode bugs live precisely at range edges; mid-range addresses never expose them.

✗ Common mistake

Assuming a decode bug will show as a data mismatch.

Why it hurts: a mis-route can land on a valid-but-wrong slave and still "work" for an address that happens to exist there; you need a routing-aware scoreboard that checks the target, not just the data.

MODULE 3

Arbitration — Round-Robin

Learning objectives

  • Explain why arbitration is unavoidable when two masters share one slave.
  • Describe round-robin mechanics and its fairness / no-starvation guarantee.
  • Read a grant timeline for two contending masters.
  • Build the checks that prove round-robin fairness in simulation.
Analogy — taking turns at a single doorway. Two people keep arriving at one narrow door. A polite rule: after one person goes through, the other gets the next turn if they're waiting. Nobody is permanently stuck behind the other. Round-robin is exactly this "you went last time, so you're after them now" rotation of priority.

Why arbitration exists

A slave port can serve one transaction's address phase at a time. When both M0 and M1 decode to the same slave in the same window, something must choose who is granted first and who waits. That chooser is the arbiter, and it sits in front of each shared resource (each slave port) in the NoC.

Round-robin mechanics

Round-robin keeps a pointer to "whose turn is next." When it grants a master, it advances the pointer to the other master. So if M0 was just served, M1 is favoured next; if M1 was just served, M0 is favoured next. The result is a fair alternation under sustained contention — neither master can monopolise the slave.

An illustrative round-robin arbiter

// Two-requester round-robin. 'last' = who was granted most recently.
// Favour the OTHER master next, so priority rotates.
module rr_arb2(input logic clk, rstn,
              input logic req0, req1,
              output logic gnt0, gnt1);
  logic last;                          // 0 = M0 went last, 1 = M1 went last
  always_ff @(posedge clk or negedge rstn) begin
    if (!rstn) last <= 1'b1;          // so M0 is favoured first out of reset
    else if (gnt0)      last <= 1'b0;
    else if (gnt1)      last <= 1'b1;
  end
  always_comb begin
    gnt0 = 1'b0; gnt1 = 1'b0;
    if (req0 && req1) begin          // contention: favour whoever did NOT go last
      if (last) gnt0 = 1'b1;            // M1 went last → grant M0
      else      gnt1 = 1'b1;
    end else begin gnt0 = req0; gnt1 = req1; end // no contention: grant the asker
  end
endmodule

Verifying round-robin — fairness is the property

Functional correctness is easy ("a grant happened"); the interesting property is fairness, and it only appears under sustained contention. So the test must force both masters to request the same slave continuously, then measure the grant distribution. Three concrete checks:

  • No starvation: assert that once a master is requesting, it is granted within a bounded number of grants (here: at most one other grant in between).
  • Alternation: under continuous dual request, grants must strictly alternate M0/M1.
  • Fair share: over a long run, each master's grant count to the contended slave is ~50%.
// No-starvation assertion: if M1 requests the contended slave, it must be
// granted before M0 is granted twice in a row. Bind this in the interface.
property no_starve_m1;
  @(posedge clk) disable iff(!rstn)
    (req1 && gnt0) |=> (req1 throughout (gnt1[->1] within gnt0[->1]));
endproperty
a_no_starve_m1: assert property(no_starve_m1)
  else `uvm_error("ARB", "M1 starved: M0 granted twice while M1 waiting")

// Fairness coverage: collect grant counts and check ~50/50 at end of test.
function void check_fairness();
  real share0 = real'(g0_cnt) / (g0_cnt + g1_cnt);
  if (share0 < 0.45 || share0 > 0.55)   // tolerance band
    `uvm_error("ARB", $sformatf("Unfair RR: M0 share=%.2f", share0))
endfunction
You cannot test fairness with light traffic. If masters only occasionally request, the arbiter almost never sees true contention, grants whoever asked, and a starvation bug stays hidden. Fairness tests must create back-to-back simultaneous requests to the same slave — a dedicated "contention storm" sequence, not the default random mix.

Activity — predict the round-robin grants

The arbiter comes out of reset with M0 favoured. The request pattern to slave S0, per cycle, is: cycle1 = {M0,M1}, cycle2 = {M0,M1}, cycle3 = {M1 only}, cycle4 = {M0,M1}, cycle5 = {M0,M1}. Give the grant each cycle (assume one grant per cycle).

Track the "last granted" pointer. On contention, grant whoever did NOT go last, then flip the pointer. With only one requester, grant that requester and update the pointer to them.
  • Cycle 1 {M0,M1}, M0 favoured → grant M0. last=M0.
  • Cycle 2 {M0,M1}, M0 went last → grant M1. last=M1.
  • Cycle 3 {M1 only} → grant M1. last=M1.
  • Cycle 4 {M0,M1}, M1 went last → grant M0. last=M0.
  • Cycle 5 {M0,M1}, M0 went last → grant M1. last=M1.

Grant sequence: M0, M1, M1, M0, M1. Note cycle 3: M1 wins uncontested and that updates the pointer, so M0 is correctly favoured at cycle 4. A subtle but common RTL bug is failing to update the pointer on an uncontested grant — which would wrongly grant M1 again at cycle 4. Your stimulus must include single-requester cycles interleaved with contention to catch exactly this.

What property of round-robin arbitration can only be verified under sustained contention?

Fairness is a property of behaviour over time under contention. With light traffic the arbiter rarely sees both masters requesting at once, so a starvation bug never manifests. You must force continuous simultaneous requests to the same slave to test it.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Write a dedicated "contention storm" sequence that drives both masters at one slave back-to-back, and measure grant share.

Why: fairness and starvation bugs only appear under true contention; the default random mix rarely creates it densely enough.

✗ Common mistake

Declaring arbitration "verified" because transactions complete in a mixed-traffic test.

Why it hurts: completion proves nothing about fairness; one master could be quietly getting 90% of the slave and the test still passes.

✓ Good practice

Bind a no-starvation SVA assertion per arbiter and a fair-share statistical check at end of test.

Why: the assertion catches an instantaneous lockout cycle-accurately; the statistical check catches a slow bias the assertion might miss.

✗ Common mistake

Only testing contention with both masters always requesting (never single-requester cycles).

Why it hurts: it misses pointer-update bugs that only show when an uncontested grant must still rotate priority — a classic RR RTL defect.

MODULE 4

Arbitration — Priority + Round-Robin

Learning objectives

  • Understand the common real scheme: priority levels with round-robin as the tie-breaker.
  • See how AxQOS can drive dynamic priority.
  • Recognise the starvation risk of priority and how it's mitigated.
  • Build verification that proves priority is honoured and no master is starved.
Analogy — a hospital triage queue. Patients are seen by urgency first (priority): a critical case jumps ahead of routine ones. But among patients of equal urgency, it's first-come-first-served in rotation (round-robin). And a good ER won't let routine patients wait forever while criticals keep arriving — there's a safety valve against starvation. This is exactly priority-with-RR-tiebreak.

The common real scheme

Pure round-robin is fair but ignores urgency; pure fixed priority honours urgency but can starve low-priority masters. Real interconnects combine them: arbitrate by priority level first; within the same priority level, use round-robin. Our NoC assigns each master a priority and falls back to the round-robin pointer when priorities are equal.

The arbitration decision, in order:
1. If only one master requests → grant it.
2. If both request with different priority → grant the higher-priority master.
3. If both request with equal priority → use the round-robin pointer (Module 3).
Priority may be static (configured per master) or dynamic (driven by each transaction's AxQOS).

The combined arbiter

// Priority with round-robin tie-break. prio0/prio1 are this cycle's
// priorities (static config or derived from AxQOS). Higher value = higher priority.
always_comb begin
  gnt0 = 1'b0; gnt1 = 1'b0;
  if (req0 && !req1)        gnt0 = 1'b1;     // only M0 asking
  else if (!req0 && req1)   gnt1 = 1'b1;     // only M1 asking
  else if (req0 && req1) begin              // both asking → resolve
    if      (prio0 > prio1) gnt0 = 1'b1;     // strictly higher → wins
    else if (prio1 > prio0) gnt1 = 1'b1;
    else begin                              // EQUAL priority → round-robin
      if (last) gnt0 = 1'b1; else gnt1 = 1'b1;
    end
  end
end

Verifying priority — two properties that pull in opposite directions

This is the crux. You must simultaneously prove two things that seem contradictory:

  1. Priority is honoured: when a higher-priority master contends, it wins. (Correctness of the priority order.)
  2. No master starves: a continuously-requesting low-priority master must eventually be served — within whatever bound the spec promises (via aging, weighted RR, or bounded preemption). (Liveness.)

A design can pass (1) and fail (2): it honours priority perfectly but never serves the low-priority master. Or pass (2) and fail (1): it's fair but ignores priority. Your test plan must check both, with separate stimulus.

// PROPERTY 1 — priority honoured: when both request and M1 has strictly
// higher priority, M1 must be the one granted this cycle.
property prio_honoured;
  @(posedge clk) disable iff(!rstn)
    (req0 && req1 && (prio1 > prio0)) |-> gnt1;
endproperty
a_prio_honoured: assert property(prio_honoured)
  else `uvm_error("ARB", "Higher-priority master not granted")

// PROPERTY 2 — bounded starvation: a continuously requesting master must
// be granted within MAX_WAIT cycles, whatever the priorities (liveness).
property bounded_wait_m0;
  int cnt;
  @(posedge clk) disable iff(!rstn)
    (req0, cnt=0) |-> (req0, cnt++)[*0:$] ##0 (gnt0 || cnt < MAX_WAIT);
endproperty
a_bounded_wait_m0: assert property(bounded_wait_m0)
  else `uvm_error("ARB", $sformatf("M0 waited > %0d cycles — starvation", MAX_WAIT))

The QoS angle

If priority is driven by AxQOS, the priority of a master can change per transaction. That multiplies the test space: the same two masters can be high/high, high/low, low/high, or low/low on any given pair of transactions. Your coverage must cross master-pair × QoS-relationship so you confirm every priority relationship was actually arbitrated — not just that "QoS was set."

The most dangerous arbitration bug is silent starvation. A NoC that honours priority but lets a low-priority master wait forever produces no error, no mismatch — the low-priority transactions just complete very late or, under constant high-priority load, never. It looks like a "performance issue" until it's a deadlocked subsystem in silicon. The bounded-wait assertion is your only reliable catch.

Activity — design the priority test plan

Our NoC lets each master's priority be set high or low (static), and S0 is the contended slave. Design the minimum set of arbitration scenarios you'd run against S0 to verify the combined scheme, and state what each one proves.

Enumerate the priority relationships: equal-high, equal-low, M0>M1, M1>M0. For each, what must the arbiter do? Which scenario tests fairness (RR) and which tests priority? Don't forget the liveness/starvation case under constant high-priority load.

Scenario A — equal priority, both storm S0: expect strict round-robin alternation and ~50/50 share. Proves the RR tie-break works.

Scenario B — M1 > M0, both storm S0: expect M1 granted whenever both contend. Proves priority is honoured.

Scenario C — M0 > M1, both storm S0: mirror of B with roles swapped. Proves priority isn't hard-wired to one port.

Scenario D — starvation/liveness: M1 high-priority requesting continuously, M0 low-priority also requesting continuously. Check M0 is still granted within the spec's bound (aging/weighted RR). Proves the low master doesn't starve.

Scenario E (if QoS-driven): randomise per-transaction QoS so the priority relationship varies, and cross-cover all four relationships. Proves dynamic priority arbitration.

A and B/C pull in opposite directions (fairness vs priority); D is the liveness safety net. Skipping D is the most common gap — and the most dangerous.

A NoC always grants the higher-priority master and a low-priority master that requests continuously is never served. Which property does this violate?

Honouring priority is correct (a). But a low-priority master that requests forever and is never served has been starved — the liveness property is violated. Real schemes add aging or weighting so even low-priority masters are served within a bound; the bounded-wait assertion catches a failure here.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Verify "priority honoured" and "no starvation" as two separate properties with separate stimulus.

Why: they pull in opposite directions; a design can pass one and fail the other, so a single test can't cover both.

✗ Common mistake

Only testing the high-priority master winning, never the low-priority liveness case.

Why it hurts: silent starvation produces no error and looks like a perf issue — until a low-priority subsystem hangs in silicon under sustained high-priority load.

✓ Good practice

When priority is QoS-driven, cross-cover master-pair × QoS relationship (hi/hi, hi/lo, lo/hi, lo/lo).

Why: dynamic priority means every relationship must actually be arbitrated; "QoS was set" doesn't prove each relationship was exercised.

✗ Common mistake

Assuming equal-priority behaviour without testing it because "they'll rarely be equal."

Why it hurts: equal priority is the path into the round-robin tie-break logic; skip it and the RR fallback ships unverified.

MODULE 5

The AXI→APB Bridge & Protocol Conversion

Learning objectives

  • Understand why P0/P1 need an AXI→APB bridge and what it must convert.
  • Read an APB transfer and the AXI-burst-to-APB-singles mapping.
  • Know the conversion hazards: burst splitting, ordering, response mapping, attributes.
  • Build verification that checks the conversion end-to-end, both directions.
Analogy — an interpreter at a border. An AXI master speaks a fast, parallel, bursty language; an APB peripheral speaks a slow, one-sentence-at-a-time language. The bridge is the interpreter: it breaks the master's long burst into single sentences the peripheral understands, waits for each reply, and translates the peripheral's "done" or "error" back into the master's language. A bad interpreter drops a sentence or mistranslates an error — and that's a bug.

Why a bridge at all

AXI and APB are very different. AXI has five independent channels, bursts, IDs, out-of-order completion, and a VALID/READY handshake on every channel. APB has a single, simple, fully-ordered transfer with PSEL/PENABLE/PREADY and no bursts or IDs. The bridge in our NoC sits between the AXI fabric and P0/P1 and reconciles these two worlds.

An APB transfer

APB moves one word in a two-phase sequence: a setup phase (PSEL asserted, address/control valid) then an access phase (PENABLE asserted, completing when PREADY is high). Read data or an error (PSLVERR) comes back in the access phase.

What the bridge must convert — and where it can go wrong

ConversionCorrect behaviourBug if mishandled

Out-of-order → in-orderAPB has no IDs; the bridge serialises and completes strictly in order.Reordered or interleaved APB transfers (illegal on APB).

Verifying the bridge — check both directions, end to end

The bridge is verified at two observation points: the AXI side (what the master sees) and the APB side (what the peripheral sees). A monitor on each, plus the scoreboard, lets you confirm the translation is faithful in both directions.

  • Forward (AXI→APB): for each AXI burst to P0/P1, the APB monitor must observe exactly N single transfers with the right addresses, write data, and direction.
  • Backward (APB→AXI): the APB slave's PREADY/PSLVERR/read-data must map to the correct AXI B or R response with the right RRESP/BRESP and a single B per write burst.
  • Error path: drive the APB slave to assert PSLVERR and confirm the master receives SLVERR — one of the most commonly broken paths.
// APB-side scoreboard check: every AXI beat to an APB target must appear
// as one APB transfer with matching address/data, in order.
function void check_bridge(axi_seq_item axi_txn, apb_txn apb_seen[$]);
  if (apb_seen.size() != axi_txn.beats) begin
    `uvm_error("BRIDGE", $sformatf("AXI burst of %0d beats produced %0d APB transfers",
               axi_txn.beats, apb_seen.size())) return;
  end
  foreach (apb_seen[i]) begin
    if (apb_seen[i].addr !== axi_txn.beat_addr[i])
      `uvm_error("BRIDGE", $sformatf("beat %0d addr exp %h got %h",
                 i, axi_txn.beat_addr[i], apb_seen[i].addr))
    if (axi_txn.is_write && apb_seen[i].wdata !== axi_txn.wdata[i])
      `uvm_error("BRIDGE", "APB write data mismatch")
  end
  // PSLVERR on any transfer must surface as SLVERR on the AXI response.
  if (apb_seen.or() with (item.pslverr) && axi_txn.resp !== SLVERR)
    `uvm_error("BRIDGE", "APB PSLVERR not mapped to AXI SLVERR")
endfunction
The bridge doubles your protocol-assertion needs. Bind APB protocol assertions (PENABLE follows PSEL, PREADY eventually asserts, no PSEL glitches) on the APB side and AXI assertions on the AXI side. A bridge bug often shows first as an APB protocol assertion firing — long before any data mismatch — which is exactly why per-side assertions are so valuable here.

Activity — predict the APB activity

M1 issues an AXI write burst to P0: AWADDR=0x2000_0010, AWLEN=2 (3 beats), AWSIZE=2 (4 bytes), INCR. The APB slave asserts PSLVERR on the second transfer only. Describe what the APB monitor should see and what AXI response the master should ultimately get.

3 beats → how many APB transfers, at what addresses (INCR, +4 each)? APB is in-order single transfers. A write burst gets ONE B response on AXI — if any beat errored, what should that single BRESP be?

APB monitor sees three single write transfers, in order, at addresses 0x2000_0010, 0x2000_0014, 0x2000_0018 (INCR, +4 each), each with its beat's write data and strobes. The bridge waits for PREADY on each before starting the next.

The second transfer returns PSLVERR. Per AXI, a write burst gets a single B response, and if any part of it errored the response should reflect that — so the master ultimately receives BRESP = SLVERR (one B for the whole burst). The bridge must still complete all three APB transfers (the error doesn't abort the burst) and must not emit extra B responses. Common bugs to check: swallowing the error and reporting OKAY, aborting after the errored beat, or emitting one B per beat.

An AXI 4-beat write burst targets APB slave P0, and the APB slave returns PSLVERR on one transfer. How many AXI B responses should the master receive, and with what status?

A write burst always gets exactly one B response on AXI. The PSLVERR on a transfer must be mapped to SLVERR on that single B — swallowing it (reporting OKAY) hides a real error, and emitting one B per beat is a protocol violation.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Monitor both the AXI and APB sides of the bridge and reconcile them in the scoreboard.

Why: only by observing both can you prove the translation is faithful; an AXI-only view can't tell a correct burst-split from a broken one.

✗ Common mistake

Only testing the happy path and never asserting PSLVERR from the APB slave.

Why it hurts: error mapping (PSLVERR→SLVERR) is among the most frequently broken bridge features and ships unverified if you never inject an APB error.

✓ Good practice

Bind APB protocol assertions (PENABLE after PSEL, eventual PREADY) on the APB side.

Why: a bridge bug usually violates APB protocol before it corrupts data; the assertion pinpoints it cycle-accurately with a clear message.

✗ Common mistake

Sending AXI bursts that the bridge or APB can't represent (e.g. exotic sizes) and treating the resulting error as a DUT bug.

Why it hurts: illegal stimulus produces false fails; constrain the generator to what the bridge legally supports, then test unsupported features separately and on purpose.

MODULE 6

Concurrency, Outstanding & Ordering Across the Fabric

Learning objectives

  • Reason about multiple masters with transactions in flight to multiple slaves at once.
  • Apply AXI ordering rules through the NoC, not just at one port.
  • Recognise head-of-line blocking and its performance impact.
  • Understand deadlock scenarios in this fabric and how they're avoided and verified.
Analogy — a busy kitchen pass. Two waiters (masters) keep firing orders to four stations (slaves). Orders for different stations cook in parallel; orders for the same station queue up. A slow station can hold up a waiter who has other orders waiting behind it (head-of-line blocking). And if two waiters each hold something the other needs to proceed, the whole pass jams (deadlock). The NoC must keep the pass flowing.

Many things in flight at once

This is where the DUT gets genuinely hard. At any instant, M0 and M1 may each have several transactions outstanding, spread across S0, S1, P0, and P1. Transactions to different slaves proceed in parallel; transactions to the same slave are arbitrated and queued. The NoC must keep every flow's ordering correct while maximising the parallelism.

Ordering rules, applied through the NoC

The AXI ordering rules (same ID → in order; different ID → any order; reads and writes independent) must hold across the fabric, not merely at a single port. Two consequences specific to a NoC:

  • Same master, same ID, different slaves: if a master sends two same-ID transactions that decode to different slaves, the NoC must still return their responses in order — even though the slaves are independent and may respond at very different times. This forces real ordering logic in the fabric.
  • Different masters: M0's and M1's transactions have no ordering relationship with each other; the NoC must not impose one (which would needlessly serialise them) nor violate each master's internal ordering.
The cross-slave same-ID case is a classic NoC bug. A master issues ID=4 to slow S1, then ID=4 to fast P0. P0 responds first. A correct NoC must hold P0's response until S1's completes, preserving same-ID order — or, more commonly, the design requires the master not to reuse an ID across slaves. Know which contract your DUT follows, and test exactly that boundary.

Head-of-line blocking

If the NoC queues a master's transactions in a single FIFO, a transaction stuck waiting on a slow slave blocks everything behind it — even transactions destined for idle slaves. This head-of-line (HOL) blocking is a performance bug, not a functional one: data is still correct, but throughput collapses. Designs mitigate it with per-target queues or multiple outstanding-ID tracking. Your verification must measure it: drive a slow transaction followed by ones to idle slaves and confirm the later ones aren't needlessly stalled.

Deadlock in this fabric

Deadlock is the most dangerous concurrency failure: a cyclic dependency where nothing can progress. In our NoC, realistic deadlock seeds include:

ScenarioHow the cycle forms
Mutual cross-traffic with full buffersM0→S0 and M1→S1 each hold the only buffer slot the other's response needs to drain.
Bridge back-pressure stalls the fabricA slow APB slave holds the bridge; if the fabric can't accept other responses meanwhile, unrelated traffic jams behind it.
Write/read interdependenceA write whose B response is stuck behind a read that itself waits on the write's resource.

Designs avoid deadlock with independent channels, sufficient buffering, and ordering rules that prevent cyclic waits. Because deadlock corrupts no data — it just stops — it is invisible to a data scoreboard and must be hunted with buffer-saturating stress plus simulation timeouts (and, ideally, formal deadlock checks).

Activity — will this deadlock, stall, or flow?

M0 issues a long burst to the slow APB slave P0 (many cycles to drain), then immediately a single read to the idle AXI slave S0. M1 is silent. The NoC queues M0's transactions in one shared FIFO per master. What happens to the S0 read, and what is the category of the problem (functional bug / performance bug / deadlock)?

The S0 read is behind the slow P0 burst in a single shared FIFO. Can it overtake the P0 transaction? Is the data wrong, or just late? Is there a cyclic dependency (deadlock) or just a queue stuck behind a slow head?

The S0 read is stuck behind the slow P0 burst in the shared FIFO, even though S0 is idle and ready. It eventually completes once the P0 burst drains — so the data is correct, just late.

Category: performance bug (head-of-line blocking), not a deadlock and not a functional bug. There is no cyclic dependency — the P0 burst will finish and release the queue — so it's not deadlock. And nothing is corrupted, so it's not a functional error. The fix is architectural (per-target queues so the S0 read can bypass the P0 burst). Crucially, a pure data scoreboard reports a clean pass here; you only catch it by measuring latency — confirming the S0 read didn't wait far longer than S0's own latency. This is why throughput/latency coverage matters alongside data checks.

A NoC returns correct data but a transaction to an idle slave is stalled behind a slow transaction in a shared queue. What is this, and how is it caught?

Data is correct, so it's not a functional bug or decode error. There's no cyclic wait, so it's not deadlock. It's head-of-line blocking — a throughput problem — and only a latency/throughput measurement (not a data scoreboard) reveals it.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Run a virtual-sequencer scenario where both masters issue concurrent, mixed-target, multi-outstanding traffic.

Why: the hard ordering, HOL, and deadlock bugs only appear when many flows are genuinely in flight across the fabric at once.

✗ Common mistake

Relying on the data scoreboard to catch deadlock or head-of-line blocking.

Why it hurts: both leave data correct — deadlock just stops, HOL just slows. You need timeouts (deadlock) and latency measurement (HOL), not data compares.

✓ Good practice

Know your DUT's cross-slave same-ID contract and test exactly that boundary.

Why: same-ID-to-different-slaves is a notorious ordering corner; the design either reorders-and-restores or forbids it — verify the actual contract, not an assumption.

✗ Common mistake

Testing each master in isolation and never both simultaneously at saturation.

Why it hurts: a single active master never creates the cyclic buffer dependencies that cause deadlock — the most catastrophic failure goes entirely untested.

The UVM Verification Environment

Learning objectives

  • Lay out the full UVM environment for the 2×2×2 NoC.
  • Decide which agents are active (drive) and which are reactive (respond).
  • Wire monitors on every port into the scoreboard and coverage.
  • Use a virtual sequencer to coordinate multi-master traffic.
Analogy — staffing a film set. The masters are the actors who drive the scene (active agents). The slaves are the responsive extras who react on cue (reactive agents). Camera operators record everything without interfering (monitors). The script supervisor cross-checks that what was scripted matches what was filmed (scoreboard). And the director coordinates everyone so the scene plays out as intended (virtual sequencer). Build the crew, then you can shoot any scene.

Active vs reactive agents

The single most important environment decision for a NoC: which side drives. The masters are stimulus sources, so their agents are active — they have a sequencer and a driver that generate transactions. The slaves receive transactions and must respond, so their agents are reactive responders — they have a driver that answers requests (returns read data, drives PREADY, can inject errors) but takes no initiative. Every port also has a passive monitor.

Building the environment

class noc_env extends uvm_env;
  `uvm_component_utils(noc_env)
  axi_master_agent  m0, m1;            // ACTIVE — drive stimulus
  axi_slave_agent   s0, s1;            // REACTIVE — respond to AXI
  apb_slave_agent   p0, p1;            // REACTIVE — respond to APB
  noc_scoreboard    sb;
  noc_coverage      cov;
  noc_vseqr         vseqr;             // virtual sequencer for multi-master coordination

  function void build_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    m0 = axi_master_agent::type_id::create("m0", this);
    m1 = axi_master_agent::type_id::create("m1", this);
    s0 = axi_slave_agent ::type_id::create("s0", this);
    s1 = axi_slave_agent ::type_id::create("s1", this);
    p0 = apb_slave_agent ::type_id::create("p0", this);
    p1 = apb_slave_agent ::type_id::create("p1", this);
    sb = noc_scoreboard ::type_id::create("sb", this);
    cov= noc_coverage   ::type_id::create("cov", this);
    vseqr = noc_vseqr   ::type_id::create("vseqr", this);
    // configure agents active/passive via config_db
    uvm_config_db#(uvm_active_passive_enum)::set(this,"m*","is_active",UVM_ACTIVE);
    uvm_config_db#(uvm_active_passive_enum)::set(this,"s*","is_active",UVM_ACTIVE); // responder is "active" but reactive
  endfunction

  function void connect_phase(uvm_phase phase);
    // Every monitor's analysis port → scoreboard (tagged by port) and coverage.
    m0.mon.ap.connect(sb.m0_imp);   m1.mon.ap.connect(sb.m1_imp);
    s0.mon.ap.connect(sb.s0_imp);   s1.mon.ap.connect(sb.s1_imp);
    p0.mon.ap.connect(sb.p0_imp);   p1.mon.ap.connect(sb.p1_imp);
    m0.mon.ap.connect(cov.analysis_export);
    m1.mon.ap.connect(cov.analysis_export);
    // Virtual sequencer gets handles to both master sequencers.
    vseqr.m0_seqr = m0.seqr;
    vseqr.m1_seqr = m1.seqr;
  endfunction
endclass

The virtual sequencer — coordinating two masters

The whole point of a multi-master NoC is what happens when masters act together. A virtual sequencer holds handles to both master sequencers so a single virtual sequence can orchestrate them — e.g. "make both masters storm S0 simultaneously" (for arbitration) or "M0 floods P0 while M1 reads S1" (for head-of-line and independence). Without it, you can only run masters in uncoordinated parallel, which rarely produces the precise contention you want to test.

class both_storm_s0_vseq extends uvm_sequence;
  `uvm_object_utils(both_storm_s0_vseq)
  `uvm_declare_p_sequencer(noc_vseqr)   // gives p_sequencer.m0_seqr / m1_seqr

  task body();
    storm_seq a = storm_seq::type_id::create("a");
    storm_seq b = storm_seq::type_id::create("b");
    a.target_base = 32'h0000_0000;   // both aim at S0 → forced contention
    b.target_base = 32'h0000_0000;
    fork                                  // run on both masters at once
      a.start(p_sequencer.m0_seqr);
      b.start(p_sequencer.m1_seqr);
    join
  endtask
endclass
Reactive slave agents are where you inject realism. The slave responders aren't just memories — they apply random READY back-pressure, variable response latency, and (on command) error responses / PSLVERR. That controlled misbehaviour is what stresses the NoC's buffering, ordering, and error paths. A slave agent that always responds instantly with OKAY hides most fabric bugs.

Activity — assign active/reactive and justify the vseqr

For each agent (M0, M1, S0, S1, P0, P1) state whether it is active (drives stimulus) or reactive (responds), and explain in one line why a virtual sequencer is necessary here rather than just starting two independent master sequences.

Who initiates transactions vs who only answers them? For the vseqr: what kind of scenario (think arbitration) needs the two masters to do something at the same instant, and can independent uncoordinated sequences reliably create that?
  • M0, M1 — active. They initiate AXI transactions; they need sequencer + driver to generate stimulus.
  • S0, S1 — reactive (AXI responders). They answer AXI requests with data/responses and apply back-pressure; they never initiate.
  • P0, P1 — reactive (APB responders). Same, on the APB side: drive PREADY/PSLVERR/read data.

Why the vseqr: arbitration and contention bugs require both masters to request the same slave in the same window. Two independent sequences run in uncoordinated parallel and only occasionally collide — you can't reliably create dense, simultaneous contention. The virtual sequencer lets one virtual sequence start both master sequences with a shared target and a fork, guaranteeing the precise concurrent contention the arbitration tests need.

In the NoC environment, what is the correct role for the S0, S1, P0, and P1 agents?

The slaves must respond to the masters' transactions, so their agents are reactive responders — driving read data, PREADY, response codes, back-pressure, and injected errors. They're not the stimulus source (the masters are) and not merely passive (they must drive the response side).

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Give every port (2 masters, 4 slaves) its own monitor and feed all of them into the scoreboard, tagged by port.

Why: routing and bridge checks need to see what arrived at each slave, not just what left each master; per-port observation is what makes the routing-aware scoreboard possible.

✗ Common mistake

Modelling slaves as instant always-OKAY memories.

Why it hurts: no back-pressure, no latency variation, no errors means the NoC's buffering, ordering, and error paths are never stressed — most fabric bugs stay hidden.

✓ Good practice

Use a virtual sequencer for any scenario requiring coordinated multi-master timing.

Why: arbitration/contention tests need both masters acting at the same instant; uncoordinated parallel sequences can't reliably produce that.

✗ Common mistake

Hardcoding the address map and widths in the agents instead of via config_db.

Why it hurts: the env can't be reused for a different map or bus width, and a map change silently breaks stimulus and checking.

MODULE 8

The Routing-Aware Scoreboard & Checkers

Learning objectives

  • Predict which slave each transaction lands on and check it actually arrives there.
  • Model each slave's memory and verify data end-to-end across the fabric.
  • Handle arbitration non-determinism — check legality, not exact order.
  • Layer protocol assertions per port alongside the scoreboard.
Analogy — a parcel tracking office. When a parcel is posted, the office predicts its destination depot from the address and logs it. When it arrives, the office confirms it reached the predicted depot with its contents intact. It doesn't insist parcels arrive in the order posted — that depends on trucks and traffic (arbitration) — only that each reaches the right place uncorrupted. The routing-aware scoreboard is that office.

What "routing-aware" adds

A plain data scoreboard asks "did the data match?". A routing-aware scoreboard asks two questions: "did the transaction reach the correct slave?" and "was the data correct there?". The first question is what catches mis-routing — a transaction that lands on a valid-but-wrong slave can otherwise slip through if that address happens to exist on both. It does this by reusing the decode() function from Module 2 to predict the target, then confirming the matching slave-side monitor actually saw it.

Scoreboard structure

`uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_m0)  `uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_m1)   // from master monitors
`uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_s0)  `uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_s1)   // from AXI slave monitors
`uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_p0)  `uvm_analysis_imp_decl(_p1)   // from APB slave monitors

class noc_scoreboard extends uvm_scoreboard;
  `uvm_component_utils(noc_scoreboard)
  // imps for all six ports (created in build_phase) ...

  // One reference memory per slave — the golden model of each target.
  bit[7:0] mem_s0[bit[31:0]], mem_s1[bit[31:0]];
  bit[7:0] mem_p0[bit[31:0]], mem_p1[bit[31:0]];

  // Per-master, per-ID expected queues (out-of-order completion handling).
  axi_seq_item expected[string][int][$];   // [master][id] → FIFO

  // Track which slave each outstanding txn was predicted to hit, for routing check.
  target_e predicted_tgt[string][int][$];

  // ---- From a master monitor: a transaction was issued ----
  function void write_m0(axi_seq_item t); record_issue("M0", t); endfunction
  function void write_m1(axi_seq_item t); record_issue("M1", t); endfunction

  function void record_issue(string mst, axi_seq_item t);
    target_e tgt = decode(t.addr);          // REUSE the Module-2 decode model
    if (tgt == TGT_NONE) begin
      t.expect_decerr = 1;                 // we expect a DECERR for this one
    end
    axi_seq_item exp = predict_data(t, tgt); // apply write / gather read from the right mem
    expected[mst][t.id].push_back(exp);
    predicted_tgt[mst][t.id].push_back(tgt);
  endfunction

  // ---- From a slave monitor: a transaction actually arrived at that slave ----
  function void write_s0(axi_seq_item seen); check_arrival(TGT_S0, seen); endfunction
  // ... write_s1 / write_p0 / write_p1 similar ...

  function void check_arrival(target_e where, axi_seq_item seen);
    // Confirm SOME outstanding txn predicted to hit 'where' matches this arrival.
    if (!any_predicted(where, seen))
      `uvm_error("SB_ROUTE", $sformatf(
        "Transaction arrived at %s but none was routed there (mis-route)", where.name))
  endfunction
endclass

End-to-end data check across the fabric

Each slave gets its own reference memory. On a write, predict_data applies the strobed bytes to the predicted slave's memory; on a read, it gathers the expected bytes from that same memory. When the master's response monitor reports completion, the scoreboard pops the matching per-ID expected item and compares — exactly the per-ID out-of-order machinery, now one layer up, spanning the whole fabric.

Handling arbitration non-determinism — the key insight

You cannot predict the exact order of arbitration, only its legality. When M0 and M1 both target S0, which one S0 sees first depends on the arbiter's state — the scoreboard must not hard-code an expected order. Instead it checks: (1) both transactions eventually arrive at S0, (2) each carries correct data, (3) per-master, per-ID ordering is preserved, and (4) the arbitration was legal (fairness / priority), checked separately by assertions and statistics.

This separation is the crux of NoC scoreboarding. Data correctness is deterministic and the scoreboard checks it exactly. Arbitration order is non-deterministic and the scoreboard accepts any legal interleaving, delegating the "was it fair / priority-correct?" question to the arbitration assertions from Modules 3–4. Mixing these up — trying to make the scoreboard predict arbitration order — is the most common way NoC scoreboards drown in false failures.

// Correct: accept any legal arrival order at a contended slave.
// We match an arrival to ANY outstanding txn predicted for this slave with
// matching address/data — not to a pre-decided "next" one.
function bit any_predicted(target_e where, axi_seq_item seen);
  foreach (predicted_tgt[mst]) foreach (predicted_tgt[mst][id]) begin
    if (predicted_tgt[mst][id].size() > 0 &&
        predicted_tgt[mst][id][0] == where &&
        addr_data_match(expected[mst][id][0], seen)) begin
      void'(predicted_tgt[mst][id].pop_front());
      void'(expected[mst][id].pop_front());     // retire within that master/ID stream
      return 1;
    end
  end
  return 0;                                      // no legal match → mis-route or unexpected txn
endfunction

Layered checking — assertions per port

The scoreboard is one layer. Alongside it, bind protocol assertions on every port: AXI handshake/ordering assertions on the two master ports and two AXI slave ports; APB protocol assertions on the two APB ports; arbitration assertions (no-starvation, priority-honoured) inside the NoC. The scoreboard catches data/routing bugs end-to-end; the assertions catch protocol and fairness violations cycle-accurately with precise messages. Together they cover the whole DUT.

The decode error check is easy to forget. For a transaction to an unmapped address, the scoreboard must expect a DECERR on the master side and confirm no slave monitor saw it (it should never reach a slave). Checking only "master got DECERR" misses a NoC that wrongly forwards the access to a slave and returns DECERR — the slave-side silence is the other half of the check.

Activity — why not predict arbitration order?

A teammate proposes making the scoreboard model the round-robin pointer so it can predict exactly which master S0 serves first and check arrivals in that precise order. Give two concrete reasons this is a bad design, and state what the scoreboard should check instead.

Think about how tightly the scoreboard would now be coupled to the DUT's internal arbiter state, and what happens the moment timing shifts or the arbiter is legally allowed more than one behaviour. What is actually a correctness requirement (data, routing, per-ID order) vs an implementation detail (which master happened to win first)?

Reason 1 — fragile coupling. Modelling the exact arbiter pointer ties the scoreboard to the DUT's internal micro-state and timing. Any legal timing variation (a cycle of back-pressure, a different latency) changes which master wins first, and the scoreboard reports a false mismatch on perfectly correct hardware. The scoreboard would break constantly.

Reason 2 — over-specification. The order two different masters are served is not a data-correctness requirement — it's an arbitration-policy property. Forcing one "expected" order rejects other legal orders. Correctness is: both arrive, data is right, each master's own per-ID order holds.

What to check instead: the scoreboard verifies data, routing, and per-master/per-ID ordering (all deterministic), accepting any legal arrival interleaving. Whether arbitration was fair / priority-correct is checked separately by the no-starvation and priority-honoured assertions plus the fair-share statistic. Keep the deterministic checks in the scoreboard and the policy checks in assertions.

When two masters contend for one slave, what should the routing-aware scoreboard check about the order they arrive?

The relative order of two different masters is non-deterministic arbitration policy, not data correctness. The scoreboard accepts any legal interleaving and checks data/routing/per-ID order; fairness and priority are verified separately by assertions and statistics. Modelling the arbiter pointer would make the scoreboard fragile and over-specified.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Reuse the single decode() model to predict the target, and confirm arrival via the slave-side monitor.

Why: it catches mis-routing that a data-only check would miss when the wrong slave happens to hold a valid address.

✗ Common mistake

Modelling the arbiter's internal state to predict exact arrival order between masters.

Why it hurts: it couples the scoreboard to DUT micro-timing and over-specifies a non-deterministic policy — endless false failures on correct hardware.

✓ Good practice

Separate deterministic checks (data, routing, per-ID order) in the scoreboard from policy checks (fairness, priority) in assertions.

Why: each tool checks what it's good at; the scoreboard stays robust and the arbitration properties get cycle-accurate, well-named assertions.

✗ Common mistake

For an unmapped address, checking only that the master got DECERR.

Why it hurts: a NoC that both forwards the bad access to a slave AND returns DECERR passes that check; you must also confirm no slave monitor saw the transaction.

Coverage, Corner Cases & a Debug Walkthrough

Learning objectives

  • Build a coverage model that proves the whole NoC verification surface was exercised.
  • Enumerate the "try to break it" corners specific to this fabric.
  • Work a realistic NoC debug from symptom to root cause.
  • Consolidate the entire course into a sign-off checklist.
Analogy — a flight test program. You don't certify an aircraft because it flew once. You sign off a matrix: every payload, every weather regime, every failure injected. NoC coverage is that matrix — every route, every arbitration relationship, every contention level, every error — recorded so "done" means "we deliberately exercised the whole envelope," not "nothing happened to break."

The coverage model

The verification surface from Module 1 becomes concrete coverage. The single most important coverpoint is the routing matrix (master × target), and the most important crosses combine routing with arbitration and contention.

class noc_coverage extends uvm_subscriber #(axi_seq_item);
  `uvm_component_utils(noc_coverage)
  axi_seq_item tr; string master; target_e tgt;
  bit contended; int arb_mode; bit[3:0] qos;

  covergroup noc_cg;
    master_cp : coverpoint master { bins m0={"M0"}; bins m1={"M1"}; }
    target_cp : coverpoint tgt { bins s0={TGT_S0}; bins s1={TGT_S1};
                                 bins p0={TGT_P0}; bins p1={TGT_P1};
                                 bins decerr={TGT_NONE}; }
    dir_cp    : coverpoint tr.is_write { bins rd={0}; bins wr={1}; }
    arb_cp    : coverpoint arb_mode { bins rr={0}; bins prio={1}; }
    cont_cp   : coverpoint contended { bins solo={0}; bins contended={1}; }
    qos_cp    : coverpoint qos { bins lo={[0:7]}; bins hi={[8:15]}; }

    // THE routing matrix — every master must reach every target (and DECERR).
    route_x : cross master_cp, target_cp, dir_cp;
    // Was each target actually exercised UNDER contention, in each arb mode?
    stress_x : cross target_cp, cont_cp, arb_cp;
    // Priority relationships actually arbitrated (when QoS-driven).
    qos_x : cross master_cp, qos_cp, cont_cp;
  endgroup

  function void write(axi_seq_item t);
    // populate fields from t and the env state, then:
    noc_cg.sample();
  endfunction
endclass
The crosses are the whole point. route_x closing means every master reached every target with reads and writes — true connectivity coverage. stress_x closing means each slave was hit under contention in both arbitration modes — the regime where arbitration bugs live. Covering master, target, and contention independently can read 100% while "S0 under contention in priority mode" was never actually exercised.

Debug walkthrough — "M1 transactions to S0 occasionally time out"

Symptom. Under a heavy dual-master regression, ~1 run in 30 reports SB_TIMEOUT on M1 transactions to S0. M0 traffic is fine. No data mismatches, no SB_ROUTE errors. AXI and APB protocol assertions are silent. The fairness statistic shows M0 getting ~85% of S0.

Step 1 — read the clues together. A timeout (not a mismatch) means M1's transactions are accepted but never completing — or never being granted. The 85/15 fair-share skew on S0 is the loud signal: M1 is being starved at S0's arbiter. Silent protocol assertions confirm the bus behaviour is otherwise legal, so this is an arbitration-policy problem, not a handshake or data bug.

Step 2 — localise: RR or priority? Check the arbitration mode in the failing seed. If it's priority mode and M0 is configured higher priority, an 85/15 skew with M1 timing out is exactly the starvation failure from Module 4 — the bounded-wait safeguard isn't working. If it's round-robin mode, an 85/15 skew is a fairness bug (the pointer isn't rotating correctly, e.g. not updating on uncontested grants — the Module 3 corner).

Step 3 — confirm with the right assertion. Enable the a_bounded_wait_m1 assertion. If it fires, you've pinpointed starvation at a specific cycle and master — far more actionable than a downstream timeout. If instead a_no_starve (RR) fires, it's the round-robin rotation bug.

Step 4 — minimal repro. Recreate with a tiny virtual sequence: M0 high-priority storms S0 continuously, M1 low-priority issues one transaction to S0, watch whether M1 is ever granted within the spec bound. A 30-line repro replaces the 30-run flaky regression and tells the designer precisely which safeguard (aging / weighted RR) is missing or misconfigured.

Step 5 — rule out the innocent. Confirm it's truly arbitration and not the scoreboard: the fair-share statistic and the bounded-wait assertion are independent of the data-matching path, so a clean data scoreboard plus a firing fairness assertion together point squarely at the DUT arbiter — not the testbench.

The discipline: timeout + fair-share skew + silent protocol assertions → arbitration starvation, not data/handshake. Check the arbitration mode to pick the right hypothesis (priority liveness vs RR rotation). Confirm with the matching named assertion, then reduce to a minimal repro before handing off. This resolves the large majority of NoC arbitration failures.

Activity — full root-cause reasoning

Using the debug method: a regression fails with SB_ROUTE ("arrived at S1 but none routed there") only when M0 writes to S0 at 0x0FFF_FFC0 with a long INCR burst. No timeouts, no fairness issues. What is your leading hypothesis and your next concrete step?

SB_ROUTE means a transaction reached the wrong slave. The address 0x0FFF_FFC0 is near the very top of S0's range (ends 0x0FFFFFFF). A long INCR burst increments the address each beat. What boundary is just above S0's range, and what AXI rule is supposed to prevent a burst from crossing it?

Leading hypothesis: a burst crossing the S0/S1 boundary. 0x0FFF_FFC0 is 64 bytes below the end of S0 (0x0FFF_FFFF). A long INCR burst increments past 0x0FFF_FFFF into 0x1000_0000+, which decodes to S1 — so later beats land on S1 while the scoreboard predicted S0 for the whole burst, producing SB_ROUTE. The 4 KB rule should prevent crossing a 4 KB boundary, and 0x1000_0000 is 4 KB-aligned — so either the stimulus generated an illegal boundary-crossing burst (testbench bug) or the NoC mishandles a burst that approaches the slave boundary (DUT bug).

Next concrete step: inspect the failing burst's address and length — compute its last byte and check whether it crosses 0x1000_0000. If it does and the burst is otherwise 4 KB-legal, the NoC is mis-handling a near-boundary burst (real DUT bug — escalate with a minimal repro). If the burst illegally crosses a 4 KB boundary, fix the stimulus constraint so generated bursts can't straddle a slave boundary, and add an assertion to flag any boundary-crossing burst so this can never silently recur. Either way, the SB_ROUTE check did its job — a data-only scoreboard would have missed it entirely.

A regression shows a timeout on one master's traffic to a slave, an 85/15 fair-share skew, and silent protocol assertions. What is the most likely root cause?

Silent protocol assertions mean handshakes are legal; no mismatch means data isn't corrupted; the 85/15 skew plus a timeout on the low-share master is the signature of arbitration starvation. The next step is to check the arb mode and enable the bounded-wait/fairness assertion to pinpoint it.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Make the routing matrix (master × target × direction) and "target × contention × arb-mode" explicit coverage crosses.

Why: these crosses are the definition of "we tested every path under the conditions that matter"; independent coverpoints can read full while the dangerous combinations were never hit.

✗ Common mistake

Declaring sign-off when tests pass but stress_x (contention × arb-mode) bins are still open.

Why it hurts: passing tests with open contention bins means arbitration was never actually stressed — the highest-risk logic ships unverified.

✓ Good practice

When debugging, combine the signals (timeout vs mismatch vs fairness skew vs which assertion fired) before forming a hypothesis.

Why: each symptom alone is ambiguous; together they pinpoint whether it's arbitration, routing, conversion, or a testbench issue — saving days of mis-directed debug.

✗ Common mistake

Never testing reset while transactions are in flight to all four slaves.

Why it hurts: reset-under-load leaves stuck arbiter pointers and leaked buffer slots — a top source of silicon bugs that steady-state tests never reach.
