MODULE 1

Why CHI Exists

Learning objectives

  • Explain the scaling limits of AXI/ACE that motivated CHI.
  • Contrast signal-based protocols with packet/flit-based protocols.
  • State what CHI is for and where you'll encounter it.
  • Set expectations for how CHI verification differs from AXI.
Analogy — phone tree vs postal service. ACE coherency is like a phone tree: to find out who has the latest news, you call everyone and wait for each to answer (broadcast snoops). That's fine for a few people. For a city of millions, you need a postal service with addresses, sorting offices, and tracking numbers — messages are packets routed through a network, and one office (the home node) keeps the master record of who has what. CHI is that postal service.

Where ACE runs out of road

ACE (Course 2, Module 8) extends AXI with snoop channels and works well for a small number of coherent managers. But it inherits AXI's signal-based nature: every channel is a bundle of dedicated wires, and coherency relies on the interconnect broadcasting snoops. As you scale to many cores and clusters, three problems bite:

  • Snoop broadcast cost. Asking every cache about every line doesn't scale — traffic grows with the number of agents.
  • Wiring explosion. Dedicated wire bundles between many managers and a central interconnect become impossible to route on a large die.
  • No native fabric scalability. AXI/ACE describe a manager-to-interconnect interface, not a scalable network of many nodes.

CHI's answer: a packet-based coherent network

CHI (Coherent Hub Interface) is Arm's protocol for large-scale coherent systems. Two big ideas set it apart from AXI:

  1. Packet/flit-based, not signal-based. Instead of driving address/data on dedicated channel wires with VALID/READY per field, CHI sends messages (packets, transported as flits — flow-control units) over a network. A request is a packet with fields, not a bundle of pins held stable across cycles.
  2. A home node tracks coherency. Rather than broadcasting snoops to everyone, a Home Node for each address keeps a record of which caches hold the line and sends directed snoops only to those that matter. This is the key scalability win.

Where you'll meet CHI

CHI is the backbone of server and high-end SoCs: Arm's Neoverse server cores, large mobile application processors, automotive compute, and anything with many coherent cores and clusters. The on-chip network that carries it is typically a mesh (e.g. Arm's CMN family — Coherent Mesh Network). If you verify CPU clusters or large interconnects, you'll meet CHI.

What changes for you as a verification engineer: AXI verification is largely about per-channel handshake legality and transaction reconstruction. CHI verification adds a whole layer: flit/packet-level field checking, transaction flows made of multiple message exchanges, and a coherency model with a directory. The coherency thinking from ACE carries over; the delivery mechanism and the scale are what's new. This course builds that mental model — it's an overview, not the full 600-page spec.

Activity — why not just scale ACE?

An architect proposes connecting 32 coherent CPU cores using ACE, with the interconnect broadcasting every snoop to all 32 caches. Give two concrete reasons this won't scale well, and name the CHI mechanism that addresses each.

Think about (a) how much snoop traffic 32 caches generate if every coherent access snoops all of them, and (b) how many dedicated wires a central interconnect needs to 32 managers. What CHI concept lets you snoop only the caches that actually hold a line? What lets you avoid wide dedicated bundles?

1. Snoop broadcast traffic. Broadcasting every snoop to 32 caches means most snoops go to caches that don't hold the line — wasted bandwidth that grows with core count. CHI mechanism: the Home Node directory tracks which caches hold each line and sends directed snoops only to those, not a broadcast.

2. Wiring/routing. Dedicated ACE signal bundles from a central interconnect to 32 managers are physically unroutable on a large die. CHI mechanism: a packet/flit-based mesh network carries messages over shared, routed links instead of point-to-point wire bundles.

Together these are exactly why large coherent systems use CHI rather than scaled-up ACE.

What is the most fundamental difference between AXI/ACE and CHI?

CHI replaces wide, held signal bundles with routed packets (flits) and replaces snoop broadcast with a home node that tracks who holds each line and sends directed snoops. That's what lets it scale to many coherent cores — it very much supports coherency; that's its whole purpose.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Carry your ACE coherency mental model into CHI, but re-learn the delivery: flits, packets, and transaction flows.

Why: the coherency concepts (states, snoops, ownership) transfer directly; assuming the signal-level mechanics also transfer leads to broken checkers.

✗ Common mistake

Treating a CHI transaction as a single AXI-style beat with VALID/READY.

Why it hurts: a CHI transaction is a flow of several messages across channel classes; modelling it as one handshake misses most of the protocol.

✓ Good practice

Start CHI verification by mapping out complete transaction flows (request → snoop → data → completion) before writing checks.

Why: CHI correctness is about whole flows and their ordering, not isolated signals; the flow map is your specification for the scoreboard.

✗ Common mistake

Trying to verify a CHI system at full scale from day one.

Why it hurts: coherency bugs are easier to isolate with 2–3 nodes; bring up the model small, then scale. Starting at 32 nodes makes every failure a needle in a haystack.

MODULE 2

Node Types & Topology

Learning objectives

  • Identify the main CHI node types and what each does.
  • Explain the role of the Home Node as the coherency point.
  • Understand how nodes connect via a mesh network.
  • Recognise which nodes a verification environment must model.
Analogy — a logistics company. Request Nodes are the branch offices that ask for goods. The Home Node is regional headquarters: it knows where every item is stored and who currently has it, and it coordinates transfers. Subordinate Nodes are the warehouses that physically hold inventory (memory). The mesh is the road network linking them all.

The node types

CHI describes a system as a set of nodes connected by a network. Each node has a role. The names use a Request/Home/Subordinate scheme, with -F (fully coherent) and -I (I/O or non-coherent) variants:

NodeNameRole
RN-FRequest Node, Fully coherentAn agent with a coherent cache that issues requests — e.g. a CPU core/cluster. Participates fully in coherency (can be snooped).
RN-IRequest Node, I/O coherentA requester without a coherent cache — e.g. an I/O bridge or a DMA-style master. Issues requests but isn't snooped the same way.
RN-DRequest Node with DVMLike RN-F/I but also handles Distributed Virtual Memory messages (TLB/MMU maintenance).
HN-FHome Node, Fully coherentThe coherency point for a range of addresses. Holds the directory/snoop filter, issues directed snoops, often integrates a system-level cache.
HN-IHome Node, I/OHome for an I/O / peripheral address region; manages ordering to that region without full coherency.
SN-FSubordinate Node, Fully coherent regionThe actual memory endpoint — e.g. the DRAM controller behind an HN-F.

The Home Node — the heart of CHI coherency

The HN-F is the single most important node to understand. For its slice of the address space, it is the point of coherency: every coherent request for those addresses goes through it. It keeps a directory (or snoop filter) recording which RN-Fs currently cache each line and in what state. When a request arrives, the HN-F decides what to do — supply from its system cache, snoop the owning RN-F, or fetch from the SN-F (memory) — and sends directed messages rather than broadcasting. Address space is typically interleaved across multiple HN-Fs so no single home node is a bottleneck.

The mesh network

Nodes connect through a mesh of routers — a grid where each router links to its neighbours. A flit travels hop-by-hop from source to destination. Meshes scale far better than a central crossbar: adding nodes adds local links rather than widening one shared structure. The trade-off is that latency now depends on distance (hop count), which matters for performance verification.

Verification implication: because address space is interleaved across multiple HN-Fs and traffic is routed over many hops, the same logical transaction can take different physical paths and interact with different home nodes depending on address. Your environment must model the HN-F directory behaviour and cover traffic to multiple home nodes — not just a single path.

Activity — assign the node types

You're modelling a CHI subsystem with: two CPU clusters with coherent L2 caches, one PCIe bridge that does DMA into memory, the DRAM controller, and the block that holds the coherency directory for the DRAM address range. Assign a CHI node type to each.

Coherent cache + issues requests = RN-F. Issues requests but no coherent cache (an I/O master) = RN-I. The directory/point-of-coherency for an address range = HN-F. The actual memory endpoint = SN-F.
  • Each CPU cluster (coherent L2) → RN-F — coherent requester, can be snooped.
  • PCIe bridge doing DMA → RN-I — issues requests but has no coherent cache to snoop.
  • DRAM controller → SN-F — the memory endpoint.
  • Coherency directory for the DRAM range → HN-F — the point of coherency that tracks who caches each line and issues directed snoops.

Note the request path: an RN-F's coherent access goes to the HN-F, which consults its directory and only then talks to the SN-F or snoops another RN-F.

What is the primary role of an HN-F (Home Node, Fully coherent)?

The HN-F is the coherency point: it owns a directory/snoop filter for its slice of addresses, decides how each coherent request is satisfied, and snoops only the caches that hold the line. The bulk data lives in the SN-F (memory); the RN-F is the requester.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Model the HN-F directory in your reference model and check it after each transaction.

Why: most CHI coherency bugs are directory/snoop-filter errors; if you don't model expected directory state you can't detect them.

✗ Common mistake

Assuming a single home node for the whole address space.

Why it hurts: real systems interleave addresses across many HN-Fs; a single-home model misses interleaving bugs and under-covers the routing space.

✓ Good practice

Cover traffic to multiple HN-Fs and across multiple mesh hops.

Why: path- and home-dependent behaviour (latency, ordering) only shows up when you exercise different routes, not one fixed path.

✗ Common mistake

Ignoring RN-I vs RN-F differences when generating stimulus.

Why it hurts: they participate in coherency differently; treating an I/O requester like a fully-coherent one generates illegal or unrepresentative traffic.

MODULE 3

The Channel Classes — REQ, RSP, SNP, DAT

Learning objectives

  • Name the four CHI channel classes and what each carries.
  • Understand flits as the unit of transport.
  • Trace a simple read as a flow of messages across channels.
  • Contrast this with AXI's five-channel handshake model.
Analogy — a courier service with four form types. Instead of one giant form, every interaction uses one of four standard slips: a Request slip ("please send me X"), a Response slip ("acknowledged / here's your status"), a Snoop slip ("HQ asking a branch: do you have X?"), and a Data slip (the actual goods). Every transaction is a sequence of these slips passed between nodes.

From channels-as-wires to channel classes

AXI has five channels that are physical bundles of signals. CHI has four channel classes that are categories of message carried as packets over the network. The distinction matters: a CHI "channel" is a logical message type, and many transactions' messages share the same physical network links.

ClassNameCarriesTypical direction
REQRequestRead/write/atomic/coherent requests — the opening message of a transaction.RN → HN
RSPResponseAcknowledgements, completion, coherency responses without data.HN ↔ RN, etc.
SNPSnoopDirected snoop requests from a Home Node to a caching RN.HN → RN-F
DATDataThe actual data payload — read data, write data, snoop data.any ↔ any

Flits — the unit of flow control

Messages travel as flits (flow-control units). A control message (like a REQ) is typically a single flit carrying the address, opcode, source/target IDs, transaction ID, and attributes. A data message (DAT) is one or more flits carrying a chunk of the cache line. Flow control is credit-based: a sender may only send a flit when it holds a credit proving the receiver has buffer space — this prevents overflow without the held-VALID-until-READY model AXI uses.

Key contrast with AXI: AXI holds a field stable on dedicated wires until VALID & READY coincide. CHI instead injects a flit into the network when it has a credit; the flit then routes to its destination. There's no "hold the address stable for N cycles" — the information is packetised and moves. This changes what your monitor samples: you capture and decode flits, not levels held across cycles.

A simple coherent read as a message flow

Watch how one read becomes a sequence of messages across classes:

// RN-F (CPU) reads a line that another RN-F holds dirty
1. RN-F  --REQ(ReadShared, addr)-->  HN-F        // request to the home node
2. HN-F  --SNP(SnpShared, addr)-->   owner RN-F  // directed snoop (directory knew the owner)
3. owner --DAT(dirty line)-->         HN-F        // owner returns its dirty copy
4. HN-F  --DAT(line data)-->          RN-F        // home supplies data to requester
5. RN-F  --RSP(CompAck)-->            HN-F        // requester acknowledges completion

What to notice: a single read is five messages spanning REQ, SNP, DAT, and RSP — and the home node consulted its directory to snoop only the one owner, not everybody. In AXI this would be an AR beat and some R beats; in CHI it's a coordinated flow. Verifying CHI means verifying these flows are well-formed and correctly ordered, on top of checking individual flit fields.

Activity — classify the messages

For a write where the requester must first gain ownership of a line, label each step with its channel class (REQ / RSP / SNP / DAT): (1) RN asks the HN for write permission. (2) HN tells other caches to invalidate their copies. (3) HN tells the RN it now has permission. (4) RN sends the write data.

"Asks for something" = REQ. "Tells a cache to do something with a line" = SNP. "Grants/acknowledges, no data" = RSP. "Carries the actual bytes" = DAT.
  • (1) REQ — the requester opens the transaction asking for write permission.
  • (2) SNP — the home node sends directed snoops telling other caches to invalidate.
  • (3) RSP — the home node grants permission/completion, no data.
  • (4) DAT — the requester sends the actual write data.

That's the four classes in one transaction — the typical shape of a coherent write flow.

In CHI, what is a "flit"?

A flit (flow-control unit) is the unit of transport: a control message is usually one flit, a data message is one or more. Credit-based flow control governs when flits may be sent. It's not a cache, a state, or a node.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Build your monitor to capture and decode flits, then reassemble them into transaction flows.

Why: CHI correctness lives at the flow level; a monitor that only logs individual flits without reassembly can't check ordering or completion.

✗ Common mistake

Modelling CHI flow control as AXI-style VALID/READY hold.

Why it hurts: CHI uses credit-based flit flow control; assuming held-stable signals leads to a checker that misreads legal traffic as violations.

✓ Good practice

Cover all four channel classes and the opcodes within each (ReadShared, ReadUnique, SnpShared, etc.).

Why: each opcode triggers a different flow; covering only "a read happened" leaves most of the protocol's behaviour unexercised.

✗ Common mistake

Checking only that data is correct, ignoring the RSP/CompAck handshakes that close a flow.

Why it hurts: a missing or mis-ordered completion response is a real bug that corrupts ordering even when the data bytes happen to be right.

MODULE 4

Coherency in CHI

Learning objectives

  • Understand the cache-line states CHI uses and how they relate to MOESI.
  • Explain how the home node and its directory drive coherency.
  • Follow how snoops change cache-line state.
  • Appreciate why ordering and hazards make CHI coherency hard to verify.
Analogy — a library with a central catalogue. The home node is the librarian with the master catalogue. Each branch (cache) can borrow a book (cache line) as "reference only" (shared, read-only) or "checked out for edits" (unique, writable). The catalogue records who has what. If someone wants to edit a book others are reading, the librarian recalls all the reference copies first. That recall is a snoop, and the catalogue is the directory.

CHI cache-line states

CHI uses a state model closely related to MOESI (Course 2, Module 8). Each cached line is in one of a small set of states describing whether it's valid, whether this cache may write it, whether it's dirty, and whether others may share it:

CHI stateValid?Writable (Unique)?Dirty?MOESI analogue
I — InvalidNo——I
SC — Shared CleanYesNo (shared)NoS
UC — Unique CleanYesYesNoE
UD — Unique DirtyYesYesYesM
SD — Shared DirtyYesNoYes (this cache responsible)O

The two axes to keep straight: Unique vs Shared (am I the only cacher → may I write without telling anyone?) and Clean vs Dirty (does my copy differ from memory → must I write it back?). A write needs a Unique state; if the line is Shared, the requester must first upgrade to Unique, which forces other copies to Invalid.

The home node drives transitions

The HN-F's directory records, per line, which RN-Fs hold it and roughly in what state. When a request arrives, the home node:

  1. Looks up the line in its directory.
  2. Issues directed snoops to the RN-Fs that hold it (to invalidate for a write, or to fetch dirty data for a read).
  3. Collects snoop responses/data, supplies the requester, and updates the directory to the new state.

Why coherency verification is hard

The difficulty isn't any single transition — it's the races. While the home node is snooping for one request, another request to the same line can arrive. CHI defines hazarding and ordering rules to serialise conflicting accesses at the home node, and getting these right is the crux of coherent-interconnect design. The space of (state × request type × concurrent-request × snoop-timing) is enormous, which is why coherency verification leans heavily on constrained-random stress plus formal methods.

The classic coherency bug: two cores write the same line "at the same time." If the home node's hazarding lets both believe they gained Unique ownership, one write is lost and the caches disagree about memory — a coherency violation that may surface millions of cycles later as impossible software behaviour. Recreating and catching this needs targeted same-line concurrent stress and a reference model that tracks the single legal serialisation.

Activity — predict the state transitions

Line X starts Invalid in all caches. Core A issues a store to X (needs to write). Then Core B issues a load of X (just wants to read). Describe the coherency actions and the final state of X in A and B.

A store needs a Unique (writable) state. After A writes, its line is Unique Dirty. Now B wants to read — memory is stale, A has the only good copy. What must the home node do, and can both end up sharing?
  1. A's store sends a REQ for write ownership (e.g. ReadUnique/MakeUnique). The HN-F sees no other holders, grants it; A's line becomes UD (Unique Dirty) after the write.
  2. B's load sends a REQ (ReadShared) to the HN-F. The directory shows A holds X dirty, so the HN-F sends a directed SNP to A.
  3. A returns its dirty data (DAT) and, for a shared read, downgrades. The HN-F supplies B and updates memory (or marks an owner).
  4. Final states (one legal outcome): both A and B in SC (Shared Clean) if the dirty data was written back; or A in SD (Shared Dirty, still responsible) and B in SC if not. Either way B got A's up-to-date value, never stale memory.

Your scoreboard must accept the legal outcomes and reject anything where B reads stale data or both remain Unique.

Before a cache can write a line, what kind of state must it hold?

Writing requires Unique ownership so no other cache holds a conflicting copy. If the line is Shared, the writer must upgrade to Unique first, which invalidates every other copy — this is what keeps the system coherent.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Maintain a full reference model of every cache's line state and the home directory; check state after each flow.

Why: coherency bugs hide in state, not data — a read can return right bytes but leave illegal state that breaks the next access.

✗ Common mistake

Verifying only one outstanding transaction to a line at a time.

Why it hurts: the hazarding/serialisation logic — where the hardest bugs live — is only exercised by concurrent conflicting access to the same line.

✓ Good practice

Use formal methods for the coherency hazard/ordering rules alongside simulation.

Why: the race space is too large to cover by random simulation alone; formal can prove ordering properties exhaustively for a line.

✗ Common mistake

Accepting only one "expected" final state when several are legal.

Why it hurts: CHI permits multiple legal outcomes (e.g. SC/SC vs SD/SC); a too-strict scoreboard reports false failures on correct hardware.

MODULE 5

Verifying CHI — What Changes vs AXI

Learning objectives

  • Summarise how a CHI verification environment differs from an AXI one.
  • Describe flit-level checking and transaction-flow reconstruction.
  • Understand the coherency scoreboard and reference model.
  • Know what CHI VIP provides and how to apply the "try to break it" mindset to coherency.
Analogy — auditing a bank vs a single shop. Verifying AXI is like auditing one shop's till: did the cash in match the cash out? Verifying CHI is like auditing an entire bank: not just each transaction, but that every branch's ledger agrees, no money was created or lost across accounts, and the order of operations never let two people spend the same balance. The scope and the consistency requirements are a different order of magnitude.

What carries over from AXI

The verification mindset is identical: try to break it, coverage ≠ correctness, assertions are your safety net, constrained-random with legal stimulus, log seeds, use timeouts. The UVM structure (agents, monitors, scoreboard, coverage) is the same shape. Much of Course 2's discipline applies directly.

Flit-level checking and flow reconstruction

The CHI monitor captures flits and decodes their fields (opcode, addresses, source/target/transaction IDs, coherency attributes). It then reassembles flows: matching a REQ to the snoops it triggered, the data that returned, and the completion that closed it — using the transaction ID and address as the key. Only once a flow is reconstructed can the scoreboard ask the real questions: did the requester get the up-to-date data, and is every cache's state legal afterward?

// CHI scoreboard skeleton — flow + coherency tracking (conceptual)
class chi_line_model;
  state_e state [int];          // per-RN cache state for this line
  bit [511:0] data;          // the line's true data
  bit dirty;
endclass

function void on_flow_complete(chi_flow f);
  chi_line_model m = lines[f.addr];
  // 1. data check: requester must have received current line value
  if (f.is_read && f.rx_data !== m.data)
    `uvm_error("CHI_SB", "requester read stale/incorrect data")
  // 2. coherency invariant: at most one cache may be Unique-writable
  if (count_unique(m.state) > 1)
    `uvm_error("CHI_SB", "two caches hold Unique — coherency broken")
  // 3. update model from the legal effect of this flow
  apply_transition(m, f);
endfunction

The crucial invariant is line 2: never may two caches simultaneously hold a writable (Unique) copy of the same line. If that ever happens, coherency is broken — and this single check catches a whole family of the most dangerous bugs.

CHI VIP

As with AXI, you'll typically use commercial CHI VIP (Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens) configured as RN/HN/SN nodes, with bundled protocol checks for flit formatting, flow legality, credit/flow-control, and coherency rules. Your job is to configure it for the right CHI issue/version and node types, drive representative and adversarial traffic, and interpret coherency failures. The protocol understanding from this course is what makes those failure messages meaningful.

The "try to break it" corners for CHI

  • Same-line concurrency: many RNs reading and writing one line at once — does hazarding serialise them into one legal order, or does an update get lost?
  • Snoop vs request races: a snoop for one transaction arriving while the snooped cache has its own in-flight request for the same line.
  • Credit/flow-control stress: starve credits on a channel — does the network stall safely or deadlock?
  • Mesh deadlock: fill multiple routers with interdependent traffic; CHI's channel-class separation and ordering rules exist to prevent deadlock — prove they hold.
  • Directory pressure: exceed the snoop-filter capacity to force evictions and back-snoops.
  • Multiple legal outcomes: ensure the scoreboard accepts every legal serialisation, not one hard-coded answer.
Bringing it full circle. Course 1 showed the SoC and why the interconnect is its heart. Course 2 made you fluent in AXI — channels, handshake, bursts, IDs, errors, AXI5 features, and ACE coherency. This course extended coherency to the scale where it needs CHI: packetised flits, a network of nodes, and a home node with a directory. The throughline is the verification engineer's creed — your job is to try your hardest to break it, and the protocol's rules are the map of exactly where to push.

Activity — design the killer coherency test

You must convince yourself a CHI interconnect never loses a write under contention. Design a test and the reference check that would expose a lost update, and explain why a naive "write then read back" test is insufficient.

A lost update happens when two writers to the same line both think they succeeded but only one survives. You need concurrency to the same line and a way to know the exact number of successful writes. A single writer reading its own value back can't reveal a race that needs two writers.

Test: N RN-Fs each perform many writes (or atomic increments) to the same line X concurrently, with randomized timing, interleaving, and snoop delays. Keep a count of the total number of writes issued across all RNs.

Reference check: model the single legal serialisation — whatever order the home node commits the conflicting writes, the number of committed updates must equal the number issued, and the final value must equal the result of applying all of them in some valid order. If the final value reflects fewer updates than were issued, a write was lost → coherency/hazarding bug. Also assert the invariant that two caches never simultaneously hold Unique.

Why "write then read back" fails: a single writer reading its own value back exercises no contention — the hazarding logic that serialises conflicting writers is never triggered, so the very mechanism most likely to be buggy goes completely untested. Lost-update bugs are fundamentally about concurrent conflicting access.

What is the single most important coherency invariant a CHI scoreboard should enforce?

Two simultaneous Unique copies of one line means two caches each think they can write it independently — the definition of broken coherency and the root of lost updates. Latency and ordering matter too, but this invariant is the bedrock correctness property.

Good & Bad Practices

✓ Good practice

Reconstruct full transaction flows before checking, and verify the "no two Unique" invariant continuously.

Why: flow-level reconstruction is the only way to check coherency, and the Unique invariant catches the most dangerous class of bugs.

✗ Common mistake

Porting an AXI scoreboard's in-order, per-ID, memory-only model straight to CHI.

Why it hurts: CHI needs per-line cache-state tracking and a home directory model; a memory-only scoreboard can't even express the properties that matter.

✓ Good practice

Bring the model up on a 2–3 node system, then scale to a full mesh once flows and coherency check out.

Why: coherency failures are far easier to root-cause with few nodes; scaling first turns every bug into a haystack search.

✗ Common mistake

Neglecting credit/flow-control and mesh-deadlock testing because "the data looked right."

Why it hurts: deadlocks and credit leaks don't corrupt data — they hang the system. They need explicit stress and timeouts, not data checks, to find.
